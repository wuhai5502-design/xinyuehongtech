Self-Service Kiosk Glossary: 33 Terms, Defined

By Mark, Project Engineer · Updated November 27, 2026

Quick answer: This glossary defines the thirty-odd terms that appear in every kiosk RFQ, datasheet and quotation — from PCAP and IP65 to MOQ, SABER and PCI PTS — in the sense they carry on a self-service terminal. Each entry is one definition plus one sentence on what it changes in a kiosk build.

Display and touch

PCAP (projected capacitive)
The touch technology behind most kiosk screens: a grid of electrical fields detects a conductive finger through a glass cover. On a kiosk it means multi-touch behind a flush, cleanable surface, which is why it is the indoor default.
Infrared (IR) touch
A frame of emitters and receivers around the display detects a finger breaking the light beams. Cheaper at large sizes, but it adds bezel depth and is more sensitive to dust and sunlight.
SAW (surface acoustic wave)
Ultrasonic waves across the glass surface detect a touch by absorption. Very clear optically, but affected by scratches and contaminants, so it is rare on unsupervised public terminals.
Optical bonding
Filling the air gap between the panel and the cover glass with adhesive to remove internal reflections. It makes a 1,500-nit outdoor screen readable where an un-bonded 2,500-nit one still washes out.
Nits
The unit of luminance (candela per square metre). Indoor kiosks use 250–500 nits; sunlight-readable outdoor screens run 1,500–2,500 nits.
IP rating
Ingress protection against solids and liquids, per IEC 60529. A kiosk is commonly IP54 (splash-resistant) indoors and IP65 on the front where it is washed or rained on.
IK rating
Impact resistance, per IEC 62262. Public kiosk glass is usually specified at IK08, with IK10 for unsupervised or high-vandalism sites.

Computing

Fanless PC
A computer cooled by conduction to its case rather than a fan. On a kiosk it means no filter to change and no airflow path for dust, at the cost of a lower sustained CPU budget.
Panel PC
An all-in-one unit with the touch display and the computer in one sealed housing. The simplest way to build a terminal: the cabinet holds a rectangle and routes one cable.
Box PC
A computer without a display, mounted inside the cabinet. Used where the display is already chosen or several outputs are driven from one machine.
Android embedded board
A system-on-module board (for example Rockchip RK3568 / RK3399) running Android, used for single-purpose content, signage and cost-sensitive builds.
LVDS / eDP
The two common internal display interfaces between a board and a panel. They matter when a board is paired with a specific screen, because the connector decides compatibility.
RS-232 / RS-485
Serial interfaces still used by industrial peripherals. A kiosk printer or reader often needs one, which decides the computer's COM-port count.
GPIO
General-purpose input/output pins on a board, used to drive simple signals such as LEDs, sensors or a cash-device interlock.
Burn-in test
Running a finished unit under load — display, readers, printer and scanner exercised in a loop — to catch early failures before shipping. Xinyuehong runs 12 hours per unit.

Identity and payment

MRZ
The machine-readable zone — the two lines of characters at the bottom of a passport — read optically by a passport reader.
NFC / RFID
Contactless short-range communication. On a kiosk it covers contactless cards, phone wallets and some ID documents, usually at 13.56 MHz.
IC card / ID card
A chip card read by electrical contact. On hospital and government terminals the ID-card reader is a separate module from the bank-card reader.
EMV
The chip-card standard (Europay, Mastercard, Visa) that governs how a payment terminal reads and authenticates a card.
PCI PTS
The security standard for payment terminals that handle PIN entry. The certification belongs to the terminal and its vendor — never to the kiosk cabinet.
EPP (encrypting PIN pad)
A separately certified device for PIN entry, with its own tamper protection. It is mounted on the kiosk, not replaced by the touchscreen.
Card dispenser
A module that issues cards — hotel keys, clinic cards, visitor badges. A hotel build depends on the lock vendor’s encoder rather than the dispenser alone.
Acquirer
The financial institution that processes a merchant’s card transactions. The acquirer approves the payment terminal and the unattended-use arrangement.

Sourcing, compliance and commercials

OEM
You define the specification and the brand; the factory builds to your document. You usually own the design.
ODM
The factory already designs the product; you adapt and brand it. The factory owns the design, and you change configuration and appearance.
White label
An existing product sold under your brand with no design change — logo, packaging and colour only.
MOQ
Minimum order quantity. On a kiosk it is often one unit on the standard module set, with higher minimums only where a new enclosure needs tooling recovered.
Lead time
The time from deposit and approved drawing to shipment. The 15-day figure on a kiosk quote covers the standard module set only; a new enclosure adds design and prototype time.
Incoterms (FOB / DDP)
Shipping terms defining who pays freight, insurance and duty. FOB stops at the port of export; DDP delivers to the buyer’s door. Comparing FOB with DDP quotes is the classic costing mistake.
SABER
The Saudi conformity system for imported electrical products: a product certificate per model and a shipment certificate per consignment, tied to the importer of record.
CE / FCC / RoHS
Product-compliance marks: CE for the EU, FCC for US radio and EMC, RoHS for restricted materials. The certificate must name the exact model being shipped.
ADA
The US accessibility law whose reach-range rules (roughly 15 to 48 inches above finished floor) decide kiosk mounting height and clear floor space.
EU CRA (Cyber Resilience Act)
EU regulation imposing vulnerability-reporting duties on connected products, including kiosks with software, with reporting windows as short as 24 hours.
