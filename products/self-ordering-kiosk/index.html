Before you request a quote

What POS or ordering software will be used?
The terminal is quoted as hardware plus platform, not as a POS. Your ordering application runs on Windows or Android and drives the screen, printer and payment device, and the cabinet and module set are then arranged around its requirements.
Is contactless payment handled by an external certified terminal?
In most projects, yes. The kiosk provides the mounting, power and cable route for a certified payment terminal, and the payment certification stays with that device rather than with the kiosk. An integrated reader is possible where your acquirer approves it.
What are the location, footprint and daily use requirements?
Counter-top units suit low counters and low volume; floor-standing units suit queues and busy sites. Give us the venue, the expected transactions per hour and the counter height, and the enclosure follows from those three answers.
Does the kiosk replace our POS?
No. The kiosk is the customer-facing terminal, and the order still lands in the POS or kitchen system through an interface. Some projects use the POS vendor’s kiosk module, others run a web app on the kiosk and post orders to the same middleware.
How many orders per hour can one kiosk handle?
A simple order takes 40 to 60 seconds at the screen, so one terminal serves roughly one customer a minute, less when the menu has many modifiers. Two kiosks usually beat one faster kiosk, because the queue is waiting for a free screen.
Can it print kitchen tickets?
Yes, with a second printer and a paper path that reaches the kitchen side, or by letting the POS print. Decide which system owns the kitchen ticket before the printer is specified, otherwise the order is printed twice or not at all.
Do we need a card reader on every kiosk?
Where customers pay at the kiosk, yes, unless one shared terminal is cabled across several units. Shared terminals slow the queue and look untidy, so most sites fit one bracket per unit.

What decides an ordering build

An ordering terminal is judged by one number: how many customers per hour can finish an order without staff help. The screen, printer and payment bracket exist to protect that number.

Counter or floor

A countertop unit suits a till point where staff still hand over the bag. A floor-standing unit suits a queue that has to keep moving while the counter is busy. Floor units cost more and take 0.3 to 0.6 m² of floor, so they usually earn their place once peak volume passes the point where the counter becomes the bottleneck.

Screen size and the menu

A 21.5-inch landscape screen matches a printed menu board and is read at arm’s length. Portrait 27 to 32 inches gives room for categories down one side and a basket down the other, which is why it dominates fast-casual. If the menu carries photographs for 80 items, portrait 32 is the calm choice; if it is 20 items and one price, 21.5 landscape is enough.

Payment: bracket or integrated reader

Most projects mount a certified terminal on the cabinet, because the approval then stays with the acquirer’s device. An integrated reader is possible where the acquirer approves it, but it couples the kiosk to one payment provider. The bracket costs a cut-out and a cable route; the integrated reader costs a certification project.

Printing and the kitchen

A receipt printer at the kiosk is standard; a second printer for kitchen tickets is not. Where the kitchen already receives orders from the POS, the kiosk only needs the customer copy. Where the kiosk is the only order entry, it needs either a kitchen printer or a POS interface that prints on the kitchen side.

Cleaning, grease and glass

Food service is the harshest indoor environment a kiosk meets. Grease films the glass, sanitiser attacks powder coat, and staff clean in a hurry between shifts. Ask for a flat glass front with no exposed bezel, IK08 cover where trolleys pass, and a finish that tolerates alcohol-based cleaner.
