Before you request a quote

What ticket size and printing technology are used?
The standard build uses 80 mm thermal stock with an auto-cutter. Pre-printed tickets, thicker stock or a two-part ticket change the paper path, so send a sample or the exact dimensions before the printer module is fixed.
What is the expected peak transaction volume?
Peak hour, not daily average, is the number that matters: it sets the printer duty cycle, the cutter rating and how often staff restock. Tell us the busiest hour and the ticket length, and the module is specified against it.
Will the terminal connect to a queue or reservation system?
Yes, where the system exposes an interface the kiosk can call. The terminal does not hold the queue logic; it issues what the system returns. Offline operation is therefore a software decision rather than a hardware feature.
Can one machine sell and collect tickets?
Yes, and most venues end up there. Build the collection path first, because that is the one that must work on the night, then add payment and seat selection around it.
What ticket sizes can it print?
80 mm thermal roll covers receipts, queue tickets and most event tickets. A4 vouchers, wristbands and card stock need a different mechanism and outlet, so specify those before the cabinet is drawn.
How do we handle refunds?
The kiosk shows the policy and routes the visitor to staff. Refunds belong in the ticketing platform, not in the cabinet, and a machine that promises a refund it cannot issue creates more complaints than it saves.
What happens if the printer jams during a rush?
Front-loading paper, a visible tear bar and a clear message on screen. A machine a steward can reload in 30 seconds is worth more than one that prints faster but needs an engineer.

What decides a ticketing build

A ticket machine is judged at the outlet: does the ticket come out, at the right size, without a jam, within the two minutes a visitor is willing to wait.

Sale or collection

A collection unit reads a QR code or booking reference and prints. A sales unit also takes payment, shows seat or time options and carries a refund path. Collection units are cheaper and narrower, and in most venues the machines end up doing both, so the collection path is the one that must never fail.

Ticket stock decides the printer

Thermal roll at 80 mm covers receipts, queue tickets and most event tickets. Pre-printed stock, wristbands, A4 vouchers and magnetic-stripe cards each need a different mechanism and a different outlet. Ask what the ticket must look like, and whether it has to survive a pocket, a turnstile and a scanner before it is handed over.

Scanning height

A QR code on a phone is read most reliably on glass at 30 to 40 degrees from vertical, lit from the side. Put the window where a standing adult naturally holds a phone, not where it is convenient for the cabinet. The same rule applies to a passport or ID slot on a collection unit: reach before aesthetics.

Duty cycle and jams

A venue selling 3,000 tickets in an evening pushes the printer far harder than an office issuing 20 queue numbers a day. Ask for the printer’s rated duty cycle and for the jam-clearing path. Front-loading paper with a visible tear bar saves more downtime than a faster mechanism, because a steward can fix it without an engineer.

Queue tickets are a different product

A numbered queue ticket needs a printer, a display and a link to the calling system; it does not need a payment device. Mixing the two on one cabinet usually produces a machine that does neither well, so decide which job the unit is for before the module list is fixed.
