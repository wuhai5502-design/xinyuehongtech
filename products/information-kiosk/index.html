Before you request a quote

Is indoor or outdoor deployment required?
Indoor is the default: a powder-coated steel body for concourse, lobby and mall conditions. For a semi-outdoor position under a canopy the front face and ventilation can be specified for dust and splash; for open-air sites we would quote a different enclosure entirely.
Which content management system will be used?
The kiosk runs your content player or a browser in kiosk mode, so the CMS stays on your side. If you have no player yet, the unit can ship with a local loop player, but the content platform is normally chosen by the venue rather than bundled with the hardware.
What accessibility and maintenance constraints apply?
Screen height, reach distance and an optional wheelchair-height second screen are set at the drawing stage. Maintenance access is from the front service door, so the unit can stand against a wall or in a row without rear clearance.
Do we need a touchscreen, or is a display enough?
A display shows a map; a touchscreen lets someone find their own destination. Once a venue has more than about twenty destinations, a searchable touchscreen pays for itself in fewer questions at the desk.
How do we keep the content current?
Pick the update route before the hardware: a CMS your team edits, a push from a wayfinding platform, or supplier-side edits. Only the first two scale beyond a handful of sites.
Can the kiosk print a map or send it to a phone?
Both are common. A small thermal printer adds a module bay, paper stock and service access; a QR code on screen costs nothing and is usually enough where the visitor carries a phone.
What brightness do we need at a glazed entrance?
Measure the lux where the screen will stand, then specify 700 nits or more above roughly 500 lux, with an anti-glare surface. Room type is a poor proxy; a west-facing lobby in summer is not an atrium in winter.

What makes a wayfinding kiosk work

Wayfinding fails for one reason: the visitor cannot find themselves on the map. The hardware only has to make that moment easy, which comes down to screen size, mounting height, brightness and who keeps the content current.

Screen size follows viewing distance

A countertop directory is read from half a metre; a concourse directory from two to three metres, often by two or three people at once. As a working rule, budget about one inch of diagonal for every 7 cm of viewing distance: 21.5 inches at a desk, 32 inches at two metres, 43 inches at three. Bigger is not automatically better, because a 43-inch panel wedged into a narrow corridor is read from too close and the map becomes a wall.

Height, reach and the second reader

The interactive zone belongs between 15 and 48 inches above finished floor. In practice that means a portrait panel centred near 1.2 m, or a sloped console at counter height where the visitor leans over it. Where two people read together, a landscape panel at eye level usually beats a portrait panel at waist height.

Brightness is the specification nobody writes

An atrium with a glazed facade can put 800 lux on a screen that was specified at 250 nits. Window-facing units need 700 to 1,000 nits with an anti-glare surface; galleries lit by spotlights need the matte finish more than the raw brightness. Ask for the measured lux at the installation point rather than the room type, and send a photograph of the space.

Who updates the map

A directory is only useful while it is current. Decide before purchase whether content is edited by your marketing team in a CMS, pushed from a wayfinding platform, or changed by the supplier on request. The third option is the one that lets a directory go stale.

What to send with the enquiry

The number of destinations on the map, the distance the screen is read from, the light level where it stands, and who will own the content after handover. With those four answers the display size, the enclosure and the update route can all be specified in one pass.
