OEM/ODM Self-Service Kiosk Project Checklist

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published October 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer: A workable OEM/ODM brief covers the transaction, the space and dimensions, the module list with interfaces, the split between branding and engineering changes, target-market compliance, and the acceptance criteria for the sample.

Write a brief that gets a real answer

A useful RFQ contains four things: what the unit has to do, what it has to fit into, how many you need and when. Everything else is detail that follows. The failure mode is a one-line email asking for "a custom kiosk price" — the reply can only be a range, and a range is not a budget.

Attach whatever exists: a sketch, a competitor photo, an industrial-design file, a measured drawing of the space. State the screen size you have in mind, the mounting position, the height available and any access constraint. If the unit replaces an existing machine, the dimensions of the old one are the most useful drawing you can send.

Enclosure: the decisions that change the price

Sheet-metal gauge, finish and sealing are the three cost drivers. A 1.5 mm cold-rolled steel body with a powder coat is the standard reference build; stainless or aluminium changes both material and welding time. A sealed front with a gasketed door adds machining and a sealing test. Colour is cheap when it is a powder-coat batch and expensive when it is a one-off custom match.

Decide early whether the enclosure is a modification of an existing platform or a new design. Modifying an existing cabinet — new cut-outs, a different module stack, a logo — is a fraction of the cost of a new shell, because the tooling, the fold programme and the fit between parts already exist. A new shell means design review, a prototype, fit checks and usually a second iteration.

Modules and interfaces

List every module with its interface and, where you have a preference, its part number. The kiosk's job is to provide mechanical mounting, power and a data path; the module's vendor provides the driver and the SDK. When a module is chosen late, its dimensions can force a cabinet change, so the module list belongs in the first brief rather than the third revision.

State the power budget too. A unit with a 32-inch bright panel, a receipt printer, an A4 printer and a cash recycler can draw well over 300 W at peak, which changes the power supply, the inlet connector and the heat load the enclosure has to remove.

Separate branding from engineering

A logo, a colour and a printed panel are branding: they change artwork and a powder-coat batch. A new enclosure, a different cooling path, an added module or a changed screen size are engineering: they change drawings, prototypes and acceptance criteria. Mixing the two in one request makes both the cost and the lead time impossible to estimate, and usually produces a quote that is wrong in one direction or the other.

Compliance belongs to the market, not the catalogue

State the destination country and the approvals the buyer expects: CE and UKCA in Europe, FCC in the United States, RoHS and REACH for materials, SABER for Saudi Arabia, and accessibility rules such as ADA in the United States or EN 301 549 in Europe. Payment adds its own layer: the terminal is certified by its provider, and the kiosk has to provide a compliant mounting and data path.

Ask for evidence per model rather than a logo on a page. A certificate that names the cabinet and the module list is worth more than a badge, and a supplier who cannot produce one is telling you something useful.

Samples, acceptance and change control

Agree what "accepted" means before the first unit is built: which tests, on which modules, with what pass criteria. A practical set is a 12-hour burn-in at ambient, a full transaction run on every module, a print quality check, a touch calibration check and a visual inspection against the drawing. Record the result and sign it off — that document is the baseline for every later change.

Change control is the other half. After sign-off, every request should be classified as cosmetic, functional or structural, with the cost and schedule impact stated before work starts. Projects that skip this step discover the cost of a late module change after the cabinets are already cut.

Timeline and milestones

A modification of an existing platform typically runs: brief and drawing review, sample build, sample approval, first batch, inspection, shipment. A new enclosure adds a design iteration and a fit-check prototype. Ask each supplier to commit to milestone dates rather than a single lead-time figure, so a slip is visible while there is still time to react.

Common questions

What should be in the first message to a kiosk OEM?
The transaction the unit performs, the space it fits into, the screen size you have in mind, the modules you need, the quantity and the target date. Sketches or a photo of an existing machine help more than adjectives. With those five items a supplier can quote a scope instead of a range.
When does a branding change become an engineering change?
A logo, colour or printed panel is branding and costs artwork plus a coating batch. A new enclosure, a different cooling path, an added module or a changed screen size is engineering: drawings, prototypes and acceptance criteria all change. Mixing them in one request makes both cost and lead time hard to estimate.
What acceptance criteria should a sample meet?
A 12-hour burn-in at ambient, a full transaction on every module, a print quality check, a touch calibration check and a visual inspection against the drawing. Sign the result off and treat it as the baseline: every later change should be classified as cosmetic, functional or structural with its cost stated first.
