How to Choose a QSR Self-Ordering Kiosk

By Mark, Project Engineer · Published November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

The short version: A QSR self-ordering kiosk is specified by three answers: counter or floor, the menu’s size, and how payment reaches the acquirer. Start with the peak orders per hour and the menu item count, then let the screen size, the printer and the payment bracket follow. A simple order takes 40 to 60 seconds at the screen, so one terminal serves roughly one customer a minute.

Counter, floor or both

A countertop unit suits a till point where staff still hand over the order, and it costs less floor space. A floor-standing unit suits a queue that has to keep moving while the counter is busy, and it earns its place once peak volume passes the point where the counter becomes the bottleneck.

The test is not the daily total but the busiest hour. A venue doing 200 orders a day but 90 between 12:00 and 13:00 needs the layout designed for that hour, because the queue forms then, not spread across the day.

Screen size follows the menu

A 21.5-inch landscape screen matches a printed menu board and is read at arm’s length. Portrait 27 to 32 inches gives room for categories down one side and a basket down the other, which is why it dominates fast-casual.

If the menu carries photographs for eighty items, portrait 32 is the calm choice; if it is twenty items and one price, 21.5 landscape is enough. The screen has to hold the whole decision, not just the list.

Payment: the bracket, not the built-in reader

Most projects mount a certified terminal on the cabinet, because the approval then stays with the acquirer’s device. An integrated reader is possible where the acquirer approves it, but it couples the kiosk to one payment provider. The bracket costs a cut-out and a cable route; the integrated reader costs a certification project.

One terminal per kiosk is the normal arrangement. Shared terminals slow the queue and look untidy.

Receipts and kitchen tickets

A receipt printer at the kiosk is standard; a second printer for kitchen tickets is not. Where the kitchen already receives orders from the POS, the kiosk only needs the customer copy. Where the kiosk is the only order entry, it needs either a kitchen printer or a POS interface that prints on the kitchen side.

Decide which system owns the kitchen ticket before the printer is specified, otherwise the order is printed twice or not at all.

Grease, cleaning and uptime

Food service is the harshest indoor environment a kiosk meets. Grease films the glass, sanitiser attacks powder coat, and staff clean in a hurry between shifts. Ask for a flat glass front with no exposed bezel, IK08 cover where trolleys pass, and a finish that tolerates alcohol-based cleaner.

Ordering questions

Does the kiosk replace the POS?
No. The kiosk is the customer-facing terminal, and the order still lands in the POS or kitchen system through an interface. Some projects use the POS vendor’s kiosk module, others run a web app on the kiosk and post orders to the same middleware.
How many orders per hour can one kiosk handle?
A simple order takes 40 to 60 seconds at the screen, so one terminal serves roughly one customer a minute, less when the menu has many modifiers. Two kiosks usually beat one faster kiosk, because the queue is waiting for a free screen.
Can it print kitchen tickets?
Yes, with a second printer and a paper path that reaches the kitchen side, or by letting the POS print. Decide which system owns the kitchen ticket before the printer is specified.
Do we need a card reader on every kiosk?
Where customers pay at the kiosk, yes, unless one shared terminal is cabled across several units. Shared terminals slow the queue and look untidy, so most sites fit one bracket per unit.
What operating system should it run?
Android suits single-purpose ordering apps and cost-sensitive fleets; Windows suits POS modules and payment SDKs that ship as Windows libraries. Choose from the SDK list your payment provider supports, not from price.
