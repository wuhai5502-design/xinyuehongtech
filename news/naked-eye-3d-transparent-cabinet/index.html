Naked-Eye 3D Transparent Cabinets: What the 10 to 100 Inch Range Actually Buys You

By Mark · Published November 15, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer: Most cabinets sold as "3D holographic" are one of three things: a transparent panel with graphics over a real object, a Pepper's ghost pyramid, or an autostereoscopic LCD. Only the third shows true depth, and it halves the resolution. Sizes run from 10.1 to 100 inches, but the panel route changes completely above 55 inches, and the brightness you can actually use depends on the light in the room.

Three different machines share one name

Search the category and every listing looks the same: a glass box, a floating object, a slogan about holograms. Open the cabinet and you find one of three things, and they behave nothing alike.

A transparent panel showcase. A transparent OLED or transparent LCD sits in the front door of a lit cabinet. The product behind it is real, the graphics sit on the glass. Depth comes from the physical object, not from the display. This is the most common format above 43 inches and the easiest to sell, because a real watch in a real box with animated text over it is genuinely striking.

A Pepper's ghost pyramid. Coated glass at 45 degrees reflects a display hidden in the base. The image appears to float in the middle of the box, and the effect holds from most angles. It is bright, cheap per unit and works in sizes from a 10-inch desk unit to a 2-metre booth, but it is a reflected image, so it has no real depth and the object inside is optional rather than essential.

An autostereoscopic panel. A parallax barrier or a lenticular lens in front of a normal LCD shows two or more views at once, and the brain does the rest. This is the only one of the three that shows true stereo depth, and the only one where the content has to be authored as multi-view. It also halves the resolution, because two images share the panel.

When a supplier quotes a "3D transparent cabinet" at a suspiciously low price, it is usually the second type. That is not a criticism. A pyramid does a better job of floating a logo than a transparent panel does, and it costs less. It just cannot do what a panel does.

The size ladder, from desk to wall

Panel availability, not demand, decides what is realistic above 32 inches. Transparent LCD exists in small sizes and is inexpensive per inch; transparent OLED clusters around 55 inches; anything near 100 inches is transparent LED film, an LED mesh cabinet, or projection onto a coated film.

Two practical notes. Below 21.5 inches, the cabinet is mostly electronics and glass, so the enclosure needs forced air or a large metal heat path or the backlight will cook itself. Above 65 inches, the unit stops being a cabinet and becomes a structure: it arrives in sections, needs a lift, and the freight can approach the panel cost.

Transparency and brightness pull in opposite directions

A transparent OLED transmits roughly 38 to 45 percent of the light behind it. A transparent LCD is closer to 10 percent, because it still needs a backlight behind the liquid crystal. That single number explains most of the difference in price and effect between the two routes.

The number that is harder to publish is contrast. A transparent panel has no black. Every dark pixel is whatever is behind the unit, seen through the panel, attenuated by its transmittance. Put the cabinet in a 750-lux retail aisle and the "floating" image competes with a bright wall behind it. Put the same unit in a dim showroom and it looks like a different product.

So the specification conversation should not start with inches. It should start with the lux on the front face. If the answer is above roughly 500 lux, plan a shroud, a dark backing panel, or a brighter panel, and expect the transparency to suffer as the brightness rises. Peak figures quoted by panel makers, often 400 to 800 nits, are measured in a dark room and are not what the customer sees at noon.

3D has a sweet spot, not a viewing angle

Autostereoscopic panels are sold on "viewing angle", which is misleading. A parallax barrier produces a limited number of views at a fixed distance. Step half a metre to the left and the image either flips to the other eye's view or goes flat. Multi-view panels widen the zone at the cost of resolution and brightness, because more views share the same pixels.

The workable approach is to design for where people actually stand. A countertop unit has one viewer at 500 to 700 mm. A floor cabinet outside a lift lobby has people walking past at 1.5 to 3 m, and a two-view panel will look broken to half of them. Tell the display supplier the standing positions, not just the room dimensions.

The cost sits in the content, not the glass

Hardware is the predictable part. A 55-inch transparent cabinet with a player, a frame and a power supply has a price you can quote in a day. The content pipeline is what derails the project.

Multi-view 3D needs an asset with depth, not a flat render. That means either a CAD model with proper materials, a photogrammetry scan, or a rebuild from product photography. The jewellery brand that wants its ring floating in the cabinet usually has a STEP file of the setting and nothing of the stone, so a modeller has to reconstruct the light return. That work is measured in days per object, and it is usually the line item nobody budgets for.

Then someone has to keep it current. A cabinet that loops the same 30 seconds for a year is a decoration. The ones that work are tied to a content management system the marketing team can actually use, with a template that keeps 3D assets inside the technical limits of the panel.

Where the format earns its cost

Jewellery and watches. The object is small, high value and looks better under controlled light. A 21.5-inch transparent cabinet at the counter, with the piece behind glass and the specification floating over it, replaces a salesperson for the browsing step.

Cosmetics and fragrance. Bottles read well in transparency. The cabinet shows the physical product and the shade range at once, which a poster cannot do.

Museums and cultural sites. Small artefacts behind glass with context layered over them. The transparency is the point: the visitor sees the real object, not a screen pretending to be one.

Automotive and industrial showrooms. A cutaway component or a gearbox rotating in a 65-inch unit explains a mechanism faster than a video on a wall.

Banks and premium retail. Gold, cards, and collectibles, usually with a lift-and-learn sensor so the content changes when the object is picked up.

Exhibition stands. The 100-inch format is a trade-show device. It is impressive for four days and expensive to move, so rent the transport, not the panel.

What to put in the RFQ

  • Panel route and generation, not just the diagonal: transparent OLED, transparent LCD, or projection film.
  • Transmittance and peak brightness, both quoted at a stated ambient light level.
  • Number of views and the designed viewing distance, with the standing positions in the room.
  • Cabinet depth, and whether the object sits behind glass or inside a sealed chamber.
  • Cooling method, service access, and how the panel is replaced if it fails.
  • Content source, player, and who updates it after handover.
  • Mounting: countertop, floor plinth, wall bracket or suspended.
  • Power and network at the position, including whether content is local or streamed.

We build these as a cabinet project rather than a panel sale: enclosure, mounting, cooling, player and the interaction layer, in sizes from 10.1 to 100 inches depending on the panel route. If the object is already in your catalogue, send the CAD file and the retail drawing, and the specification conversation gets much shorter.

Common questions

Is a transparent 3D cabinet a real hologram?
No. Nothing in a commercial cabinet reconstructs a light field the way a hologram does. What ships is a transparent panel with an autostereoscopic layer, or a Pepper's ghost pyramid using coated glass and a projector. Both produce a convincing floating image. Neither is holography, and a supplier who explains which one they use is usually the one worth talking to.
Why does the picture look dim in a bright shop?
A transparent panel splits its light between the image and whatever is behind it. The open area lets ambient light through, and that light lifts the black level, so contrast falls as the room gets brighter. Under 300 lux it looks impressive. Under direct shop lighting the image competes with the object behind the glass and usually loses.
Can one cabinet show both 2D video and the 3D effect?
Yes, on an autostereoscopic panel: the 3D layer is switched off for 2D content, which then plays at full resolution. On a lenticular panel the lens stays in the optical path, so 2D content keeps a visible texture pattern. If the cabinet has to run brand video most of the day and 3D some of the time, ask for a switchable parallax barrier.
