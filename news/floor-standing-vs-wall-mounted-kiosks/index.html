Floor-Standing vs Wall-Mounted Kiosks

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published October 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer: Floor-standing units suit queue-forming transactions and carry the full module set; wall-mounted units save corridor width but hold only shallow modules and need a structural wall. Decide by footprint, viewing height, module space and how often staff must change paper.

Start with the footprint, not the cabinet

A floor-standing terminal needs a footprint of roughly 350 by 450 mm for a single-screen unit and 500 by 600 mm once a printer, a document reader and a cash module share the base. The number that actually decides the position is the clear space in front: a wheelchair user needs a 30 by 48 inch approach, and a standing queue needs about 1.2 m from the screen to the next person's toes. Measure that before the cabinet is drawn; it eliminates more positions than the cabinet dimensions ever will.

A wall-mounted unit is a different calculation. The cabinet is typically 100 to 250 mm deep, so the corridor keeps its width, but the wall has to carry 40 to 90 kg at an eccentric load. That means blocking or a steel sub-frame behind the finish, not plasterboard anchors. If the wall is a glazed partition or a drywall demountable system, budget for a frame and a structural check.

Viewing height decides the screen size

ADA 308 and 309 ask for controls within 15 to 48 inches above the finished floor; a comfortable screen centre for a standing adult lands around 1,200 to 1,400 mm. A floor-standing cabinet reaches that height with a stand, which is why a 27-inch portrait screen reads comfortably while a 21.5-inch landscape does not. A wall-mounted unit usually sits at 1,100 to 1,200 mm centre and needs a shallower viewing cone — fine for a 21.5-inch screen at 500 to 700 mm viewing distance, tight for anything larger.

Two practical consequences follow. On a floor-standing unit, spend the height budget on the screen and put the printer outlet below 1,200 mm so the ticket is reachable without bending. On a wall-mounted unit, keep the reading and printing tasks separate: put the printer in a pedestal or a counter below the screen rather than hanging it at shoulder height.

Where the hardware goes

Inside a floor-standing cabinet there is room for the computing platform, a receipt printer, a document or card module, a UPS and the cable slack that service work needs. That space is the real reason floor-standing units dominate multi-function kiosks: a 32-inch terminal with five modules needs a cabinet volume a wall unit cannot offer.

A wall-mounted unit is limited to shallow modules — a card reader, a QR scanner, a contactless pad, a small thermal printer. When the computing platform will not fit, it goes into a ceiling void, a riser cupboard or a nearby comms cabinet, and the run to the screen becomes a design constraint: keep USB and video runs under the limits your platform supports, and specify shielded cable if the run passes near mains wiring.

Service access and consumables

Every kiosk needs someone to change paper, clear a jam, wipe the screen and reboot it. On a floor-standing unit, a front-hinged door with a lock is the default; a rear door only works if the unit stands away from the wall, which conflicts with queue geometry more often than expected. On a wall-mounted unit, the service door usually swings forward, so the same 900 mm of clear floor that serves the wheelchair user serves the technician.

Count the consumable events before choosing the style. A busy ticket counter may reload a printer three times a day; a check-in terminal once a week. High-consumable tasks favour a floor-standing cabinet with a large roll and a front door. Low-consumable tasks — a directory, a card enquiry — tolerate a wall unit.

Traffic pattern and queue geometry

Floor-standing terminals form queues well because people can approach from three sides and the screen faces the aisle. Wall-mounted terminals serve better as side tasks: a payment at the end of a counter, a check-in in a corridor, a wayfinding point beside a lift lobby. The failure mode for a wall unit is a queue that forms along the wall and blocks the corridor behind it.

Watch the approach angle too. A screen set flat against a wall is read at 90 degrees, which is fine for a short interaction and tiring for a two-minute form. A floor-standing cabinet can be rotated five to ten degrees toward the approach path, which costs nothing at drawing stage and removes a whole class of complaints.

Installation cost and structure

Floor-standing units cost more to ship and less to install: a plinth or levelling feet, a power point and a network drop. Wall-mounted units cost less to ship and more to install: a structural survey, blocking, a licensed installer, and a lift if the wall is above ground floor. On a multi-site roll-out the difference compounds — a wall unit that needs a survey per site can add weeks to a schedule.

Common questions

Can a wall-mounted kiosk be fitted to plasterboard?
Not directly. A loaded wall unit weighs 40 to 90 kg at an eccentric distance from the wall, so the fixings have to reach blocking, a steel sub-frame or a structural wall. Plasterboard anchors are rated for a fraction of that load. If the wall is a demountable partition, budget for a frame behind it.
How much clear floor space does a kiosk need?
A 30 by 48 inch clear floor area in front of the controls covers wheelchair approach under ADA 305. Practically, allow about 1.2 m from the screen face so a queue does not press against the current user, and keep 900 mm clear for whoever changes the paper.
Which style is cheaper for a multi-site roll-out?
Wall-mounted units are cheaper to ship but usually dearer to install, because each site needs a wall survey and blocking before the unit arrives. Floor-standing units ship on a pallet and need a plinth and two services. Once a site has more than a handful of units, the floor-standing route tends to win on schedule.
