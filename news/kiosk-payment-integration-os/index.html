Integrating a Payment Terminal into a Kiosk

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published November 10, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer: Mount the terminal on the cabinet rather than building payment into it. The terminal keeps its own PCI PTS and scheme approval; the kiosk supplies power, a cut-out for the exact device model and a serial, USB or ethernet link. Choose the operating system from the SDK your provider supports — Windows has the widest coverage, Android is the cheapest, Linux the most locked down.

Payment is the part of a kiosk project that most often arrives late, and it is the part that cannot be retrofitted cheaply. The terminal is a certified device with its own approval, the kiosk is the host that powers it, presents it and talks to it — and the boundary between those two responsibilities decides the bracket, the cable route and the operating system.

The short version

Mount the terminal on the cabinet rather than building payment into the cabinet. Cut the bracket for the exact device model, route power and data inside the enclosure, and let the terminal's own certification stay with the terminal. Then choose the operating system by asking which SDK your payment provider supports, not by asking which platform is cheapest.

Who certifies what

Three different things get called "payment certification" and they belong to different parties:

  • The terminal carries its own approval — PCI PTS for PIN entry devices, EMVCo for chip behaviour, and the scheme rules for unattended use. That approval is held by the terminal vendor and the acquirer, not by the kiosk factory.
  • The payment application running on the terminal, and the key injection process, belong to the provider or the acquirer. The kiosk never sees a PIN block.
  • The kiosk is a host: it supplies power, a mounting point, a data link and a physical position that lets the customer reach the device privately. Its job is mechanical and electrical, not cryptographic.

That division is why a kiosk quote normally excludes the payment device. Where a project does integrate a reader into the cabinet face, the certification scope changes and the schedule usually does too.

Three ways to connect the terminal

Connection methods used in kiosk projects. The right choice follows from your provider's SDK, not from the cable you prefer.

MethodTypical deviceWhat the kiosk application doesTrade-off
Serial (RS-232) with a vendor protocolCountertop terminal from Ingenico, Verifone or PAXSends the amount and receives the result over a documented protocol; the terminal drives its own UIVery stable, well understood, but the protocol and cable are device-specific
USB or serial with a provider SDKTerminal supplied by a PSP with a local SDKCalls the SDK from the kiosk app; the SDK handles the transaction and the terminal screenCleaner integration, but the SDK ties you to one provider and often one OS
Ethernet or cloud (semi-integrated)Terminal with its own network linkOnly displays the amount and polls the result; the terminal talks to the PSP directlyLeast coupling and easiest to swap providers, at the cost of a second network drop

Serial is still the most common in unattended kiosks because it survives reboots and needs no driver stack. The provider SDK route is faster to build and harder to leave. The semi-integrated route is the easiest to certify, because the kiosk never handles card data at all.

The bracket is a drawing, not an accessory

Every terminal has a footprint, a cable exit position, a screen angle and a PIN pad that needs a sight line the next customer cannot share. In practice the mounting drawing fixes four things:

  • The cut-out. A plate cut for an Ingenico model will not accept a Verifone of the same class. The drawing is made against the device datasheet and confirmed before the sheet metal is cut.
  • The reach. The terminal has to sit inside the same reach envelope as the screen. On an accessible build that means the card slot and the PIN pad both fall within the 15 to 48 inch band described in the ADA and IP rating guide.
  • The cable route. Power and data enter through the cabinet, not through a hole drilled on site. A visible cable is both a tamper opportunity and a service liability.
  • The service access. Paper, card stock and the terminal itself have to be reachable by staff without removing the kiosk from the wall or the plinth.

Re-cutting a finished fascia is the single most expensive change in a kiosk project. It is worth waiting a week for the terminal model before releasing the drawing.

When you need a reader on the kiosk as well

Payment is not the only card interaction. Loyalty cards, staff badges, national ID and access cards are read by the kiosk, not by the payment terminal, and mixing the two confuses customers. The common arrangement is a reader bay on the kiosk for identification, and a separate certified terminal for payment, positioned so the customer does not present the same card twice to two different slots.

Choosing the operating system

Platform comparison for kiosk applications. Support status refers to the application stack, not to the hardware.

PlatformBest fitStrengthsConstraints
Windows (IoT Enterprise LTSC)Applications with a full driver stack, OPOS/JavaPOS peripherals, provider SDKs that ship as Windows librariesWidest driver and SDK support; familiar to integratorsLicence cost, larger update surface, needs a locked-down shell
Android (AOSP)Content, ordering and check-in apps with a small peripheral setCheap hardware, fast boot, simple kiosk modeFewer payment SDKs; serial device support depends on the board vendor
LinuxLocked-down deployments where the application owns the whole boot chainSmallest attack surface, no licence cost, full controlDrivers must be sourced per device; support is on you

The decision is usually made by one question: does your payment provider ship an SDK for that platform? A platform that cannot talk to the terminal is not a candidate, however good its price. The second question is who maintains the image in the field — remote update tooling differs sharply between the three.

What to settle before the drawing

  • Terminal make, model and mounting drawing, or a decision to use a universal bay
  • Connection method and the protocol or SDK version the application will use
  • Whether the terminal needs its own network drop or shares the kiosk link
  • Operating system, chosen against the SDK list rather than the price list
  • Peripheral interfaces: serial, USB or both, and how many of each
  • Cash: none, acceptor only, or acceptor with recycler, and the cabinet depth that implies
  • Remote update and monitoring tooling, and who operates it
  • Key injection and terminal deployment process, owned by the provider

Questions integrators ask first

Can we mount our own payment terminal on the kiosk?
Yes, and it is the normal arrangement. The bracket and cut-out are drawn for the specific terminal model, with power and data routed inside the cabinet. Send the device model and its installation drawing before the sheet metal is cut, because a plate made for one vendor's device will not accept another's.
Does the kiosk need its own PCI certification?
The payment terminal carries the PIN entry approval, and the payment application belongs to your provider. The kiosk is the host: it supplies mounting, power and the data link, and it should never handle card data. If a reader is integrated into the cabinet face instead of a separate terminal, the certification scope changes and that is a different project.
Which operating system should the kiosk run?
Whichever platform your payment provider supports with an SDK. Windows has the widest driver and SDK coverage; Android is cheaper and simpler for content and ordering apps; Linux gives the smallest attack surface where the application controls the whole boot chain. Price is the wrong first question.
Do we need a separate card reader for loyalty or ID?
Usually yes, and it is better to keep it physically separate from the payment terminal. Loyalty, staff and ID cards are read by the kiosk application, while payment is handled by the certified terminal. Two slots in different positions also stop customers presenting the same card twice.
Can the kiosk accept cash?
A cash acceptor or recycler can be fitted, but it changes the cabinet depth, the service access and the certification scope. Card-only builds are smaller and faster to certify; cash should be a deliberate decision, not a default.
How long does it take to change the payment device after production?
The bracket is the constraint, not the software. Swapping to a device with the same footprint is quick; a different footprint means a new front plate, and on a finished cabinet that is a re-cut. This is why the terminal model is fixed before the drawing is released.
