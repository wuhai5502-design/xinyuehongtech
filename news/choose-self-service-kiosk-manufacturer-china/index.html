How to Choose a Self-Service Kiosk Manufacturer in China

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published November 20, 2026 · 14 min read

Quick answer: A self-service kiosk manufacturer builds the enclosure, mounts the printer, scanners and card readers, loads the operating-system image and tests the assembled terminal. In China the three supplier types are sheet-metal factory, integrator and trading company, and two questions tell them apart. A 1-unit MOQ covers labour and programming, not tooling. A 15-day lead time starts after deposit and drawing approval. And payment certification belongs to the terminal vendor and the acquirer, never to the kiosk cabinet.

A self-service kiosk is a mechanical product wrapped around someone else’s software. That single fact defines the job, and it defines the boundary of what a manufacturer can be held to. The scope is the part a software team cannot fix from a laptop: the cabinet that has to survive a public lobby, the module stack that has to be reachable and serviceable, the computing platform that must still boot after a 12-hour thermal soak, and the cable routing that has to pass an electrician’s inspection.

What a self-service kiosk manufacturer is responsible for

In practical terms, a full-scope kiosk manufacturer owns five things.

  • Enclosure engineering. Sheet-metal design, material choice, powder-coat finish, service access and cable management.
  • Module integration. Mounting and wiring the printer, scanner, camera, card readers, PIN pad and payment-terminal bracket as one assembly.
  • Platform selection. Matching x86 or Arm boards to the operating system, the I/O count and the environment.
  • Build and test. Assembly, burn-in, final inspection and packing.
  • Documentation. Drawings, module list, test record and packing list.

Anything outside that list is either your job or a separate supply contract. A kiosk factory will not certify your payment flow, will not write your ordering app and, correctly, will not promise that an uncertified reader works with an acquirer.

Factory, integrator, trader: the three supplier types

The supplier type decides what you can change and who carries the risk when a drawing moves. It is usually visible in the second answer, not the first.

None of these is automatically wrong. A trading company can be the right answer when you need twenty different SKUs consolidated, and an integrator can be excellent at niche module stacks. What is wrong is buying factory pricing and factory accountability from a company that does not have a factory. The audit checklist further down settles the question in a single call.

Question 5 is the one buyers most often get wrong. “CE and FCC” is a claim, not evidence. The certificate has to name the model you are buying, because a kiosk with a different screen, a different PC and a different printer is a different product from a compliance point of view.

What a 1-unit MOQ actually means

A minimum order of one unit is common among Shenzhen kiosk builders, and it is misunderstood in both directions. It does not mean the factory will invent a new cabinet for the price of a standard one, and it does not mean the quote is a loss leader.

MOQ 1 is a statement about labour and programming, not about tooling. A sheet-metal shop can cut one set of panels, bend them on the same CNC press brake it uses for a batch of fifty, and send a single unit through the same assembly and burn-in route. What it cannot do for free is build a new enclosure from a blank page, make new forming tools, or match a custom powder-coat colour in a batch smaller than the coating line’s minimum load.

So the useful MOQ question is not “what is your minimum”, but: which parts of my configuration are standard, which are modified, and which require new tooling, and what is the smallest order for each?

The answer usually comes back in three tiers: standard module set with MOQ 1, a modified cabinet from an existing design at a small batch, and a new enclosure where tooling and setup are recovered over a stated volume. Any factory that quotes without that split will surprise you later, usually at the invoice.

What a 15-day lead time covers

Published lead times are usually accurate and usually narrower than buyers assume. The 15-day figure that appears on many Chinese kiosk sites refers to production inside the standard module set, counted from deposit plus an approved drawing or sample. It is a production number, not a project number.

Two rules follow. First, the production clock cannot start before the drawing is approved, so every day spent on artwork and sign-off is a day added to the end, not absorbed in the middle. Second, validation is the stage buyers underestimate, because it is the only stage that depends on someone other than the factory: your application, your SDK, your payment provider.

Ask any supplier to put dates against these seven stages in writing. A factory that will only commit to a single number for the whole project has not understood the project.

Certification, payment, and the boundary nobody explains

Three separate approval systems sit around a kiosk, and confusing them is the most expensive mistake in this category.

Product compliance: CE, FCC, RoHS and market schemes

These apply to the assembled product and to what is inside it. A kiosk built for the EU needs CE marking supported by the right directive testing; a unit shipped to Saudi Arabia needs SABER conformity documents; a US deployment needs FCC compliance for the radio and computing parts. Certification scope is per model and per destination, and it belongs in the quotation rather than in a footer logo.

Accessibility and physical ratings

ADA reach ranges, roughly 15 to 48 inches above finished floor for the operable parts, plus IP ratings for dust and water and IK classes for the cover glass, are specification items rather than marketing lines. They change the drawing: mounting height changes the cabinet, IP65 changes the sealing and the ventilation path, IK08 changes the glass.

Payment approval: PCI PTS and scheme certification

This is the one manufacturers cannot own. A card reader or PIN pad is certified as a device, and the acquirer approves the merchant’s integration. The kiosk’s job is mechanical and electrical: a cut-out that fits the terminal, a mounting bracket, a power and cable route, and enough space for a customer to reach the keypad comfortably. Where a project needs an integrated reader, that decision belongs to the payment provider and the kiosk drawing follows it.

A useful test in any supplier conversation: ask them to describe where their responsibility ends on a card transaction. The good answer draws a line at the terminal connector. The bad answer draws it nowhere.

Red flags worth walking away from

  • A price with no module list. If the printer model is not named, it is not specified.
  • Certification claimed for a model number you have not seen on a certificate. Ask for the PDF.
  • No burn-in record, only “we test 100%”. A record costs nothing to produce if the test exists.
  • A promise to certify your payment flow. Nobody outside the payment chain can make that promise.
  • A drawing that arrives after the deposit. Design is the cheap part; it should come first.
  • A lead time quoted without a start condition. “15 days” means nothing without “from deposit and approved drawing”.
  • No answer on end-of-life. A kiosk lives five to eight years; PC platforms do not.

What to send in a first inquiry

A good inquiry is short and specific. It gets a useful reply in a day instead of a brochure.

  • Application. What the customer does at the terminal, in one sentence.
  • Venue and conditions. Indoor or outdoor, footfall, ambient temperature, floor space or wall space.
  • Peripherals. Printer, scanner, camera, card readers, ID reader, payment terminal model.
  • Software. Operating system, application, SDK or API constraints, who owns updates.
  • Reference design. A photo, a sketch or a link. Often faster than a written spec.
  • Volume and schedule. Pilot quantity, expected annual volume, target installation date.
  • Market and certification. Destination country and the schemes that apply.
  • Commercial terms. Incoterm, destination port, who handles import clearance.

Send the list even if half the answers are “not decided yet”. Naming the open questions is itself a useful signal to the supplier, and it usually shortens the requirement review.

Questions buyers ask most

What does a self-service kiosk manufacturer actually make?
It makes the enclosure, integrates the printer, scanners, card readers and payment bracket, installs the computing platform and operating system image, and tests the assembled terminal. It does not normally write your application software or certify your card payments.
What is a realistic MOQ for a custom kiosk?
One unit is realistic when the configuration stays inside a standard module set and an existing cabinet design. A modified cabinet typically needs a small batch, and a new enclosure needs a volume that recovers tooling and setup costs. Ask for the three-tier split in writing.
How long does a custom kiosk take to build?
Production inside the standard module set commonly runs about 15 days from deposit and drawing approval. A new enclosure or a new module stack adds design, tooling, prototype and validation time before that clock starts, so the project date is always longer than the production date.
Can a kiosk manufacturer work with software I already use?
Usually yes, if the interfaces are documented. Send the operating system, application, driver list and SDK requirements early. Payment flows that involve card schemes stay with your certified provider, with the kiosk handling the physical mounting and cabling.
Who certifies card payments on a kiosk?
The payment terminal vendor holds the device certification and your acquirer approves the merchant integration. The kiosk manufacturer provides the cut-out, bracket, power and cable route. No cabinet maker can certify a payment flow it does not operate.
What certifications should a kiosk carry?
For the EU, CE marking with the relevant directive testing; for the US, FCC compliance for the radio and computing parts; RoHS for material restrictions. Destination-specific schemes apply in some markets, such as SABER for Saudi Arabia. Confirm the certificate names the exact model you are buying.
How do I verify a factory I cannot visit?
Audit by document and video call: the cut sheet with your drawing number, the bending tooling, the coating batch record, a burn-in log with a serial number, inspection sheets and an RMA procedure. A refusal on any of those is more informative than a tour.
What should I send in a first inquiry?
The application, venue, peripheral list, software stack, a reference photo, estimated volume, target date and destination market. Open questions can be listed as open. That is enough for a configuration recommendation and a first quotation.

Standards and further reading

The numbers in this guide come from published standards rather than supplier habit: ADA 308 and 309 for reach ranges on public terminals, IEC 60529 for IP ratings, IEC 62262 for IK impact classes, PCI PTS for payment-terminal security, and the EU Cyber Resilience Act for vulnerability reporting on connected kiosks. Ask any supplier which revision of each standard their drawing was checked against, and put the answer in the specification.
