Hotel Check-in Kiosk Hardware: Peripherals and Enclosure

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published November 10, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer: Four peripherals decide a hotel check-in build — passport or ID reader, key card dispenser with a lock-vendor encoder, camera and receipt printer — plus a mounted payment terminal. The enclosure is 1.2 to 2.0 mm cold-rolled steel, laser cut and CNC bent, powder coated at 60 to 80 microns, and takes three to six weeks from approved CAD to a finished batch.

A hotel check-in kiosk looks like a software project and is actually a peripheral-integration project. The screen is settled in a week; the key card encoder, the passport reader and the lock vendor decide whether the unit works at all. Then the enclosure has to fit all of it, survive a lobby, and look like it belongs to the brand.

The short version

Four peripherals decide the build: a passport or ID reader, a key card dispenser with an encoder that matches the lock system, a camera for identity capture, and a receipt printer. The payment terminal is mounted, not integrated. The enclosure is 1.2 to 2.0 mm cold-rolled steel, laser cut and CNC bent, powder coated at 60 to 80 microns, and it takes three to six weeks from approved CAD to a finished batch — one to two weeks for the drawing, two to three for a prototype.

Part 1: the peripheral stack

What each peripheral needs, and where hotel projects usually stall.

The key card problem is a vendor problem

Most hotel self-service projects fail here, and not because the hardware is bad. A key card is issued by an encoder that must be certified or licensed by the lock system vendor — ASSA ABLOY, Salto, Onity and Dormakaba all have their own encoder families and their own integration paths. The kiosk factory can provide the bay, the power and the serial link, but it cannot make a third-party encoder talk to a lock system without that vendor in the room.

The practical sequence is: lock vendor first, encoder model second, kiosk bay third. Projects that start with the cabinet usually end up with a dispenser that issues blank cards, which is worse than no kiosk at all.

Identity capture is a policy question as much as a hardware one

Reading a passport MRZ gives you name, number and dates. Reading the chip gives you a signed record. Scanning the photo page gives you an image that a human may have to review. Which of those the property is allowed to store, and for how long, is set by local data-protection rules and by the hotel group's own policy — and it changes the reader specification. A property that only needs to verify a name does not need a chip reader.

Part 2: from CAD to powder coating

An enclosure is a sequence of processes, and each one has a tolerance that accumulates. The chain for a kiosk cabinet is: laser cutting, CNC bending, welding or riveting, surface pretreatment, powder coating, then assembly and test.

Cutting and bending

Sheet is normally 1.2 to 2.0 mm cold-rolled steel for a painted cabinet, or 1.5 mm stainless where the front face is touched constantly and must not chip. Laser cutting holds roughly ±0.1 mm on a profile; CNC bending holds roughly ±0.2 mm on a bend position, and the tolerance grows with the number of bends in a chain. That is why a drawing that stacks six bends to position a card slot is a drawing that will need a slotted hole, not a round one.

Finishing

Powder coating is where appearance and durability are decided. The pretreatment matters more than the powder: degreasing, phosphating and a rinse before coating is what stops a cabinet from rusting at a scratch two years later. Coating thickness is typically 60 to 80 microns, and colour is matched against a physical sample rather than a screen, because the same RAL number looks different in a lobby at 3000 K and in a factory under daylight.

Prototype, then batch

Typical schedule for a custom enclosure, counted from the approved brief.

StageDurationWhat is fixed at the end of it

Prototype build2 – 3 weeksFit of every module, coating sample, label positions
Validation1 – 2 weeksBurn-in, thermal check, customer sign-off on the sample
Production batch3 – 4 weeksFixtures and bending programs reused, batch coating, final inspection

The economics change between prototype and batch. A single unit carries the full programming and setup cost; a batch of fifty amortises the same work across the run. What does not amortise is new tooling — a weld fixture or a custom extrusion is a one-off that stays a one-off, and it is quoted separately for that reason.

What drives cost in the enclosure

  • Number of bends and welds. Every additional bend is a setup and a tolerance, and visible welds need grinding and re-finishing.
  • Material. Stainless costs two to three times cold-rolled steel per kilogram, before the extra tool wear.
  • Finish. A standard powder colour is cheap; a colour match to a brand guideline, or a brushed stainless finish, is not.
  • Cut-outs and apertures. Each peripheral opening is a separate laser path, and a mis-sized one is a re-cut.
  • Service access. A hinged front door with a lock and a gasket costs more than a screw-fixed panel, and is usually worth it.
  • Volume. Fifty units against one typically removes 15 to 25% of the unit price, mostly in programming and coating batches.

Specification checklist before an RFQ

  • The full peripheral list, with model numbers where already chosen
  • Lock system and encoder, confirmed with that vendor in writing
  • Documents the reader must accept, and what may be stored
  • Cabinet material, colour reference and finish (painted, stainless, brushed)
  • Mounting: floor-standing with plinth, wall bracket, or countertop
  • Ingress and impact requirements, which decide gaskets and glass (see the ADA, IP and IK guide)
  • Branding: logo file, placement, and whether it is printed, etched or a badge
  • Prototype quantity, batch quantity, and the date the first unit has to be on site

Questions hotel projects ask first

Can the kiosk issue a hotel key card?
The cabinet can carry a dispenser and an encoder, but the encoder must be compatible with the lock system and is normally licensed by that vendor. The sequence that works is lock vendor first, encoder model second, kiosk bay third. Without the lock vendor's involvement the unit can dispense a card but not a working key.
Which documents should the reader accept?
That depends on the property and the market. A city hotel may need passport MRZ plus national ID; a resort may need residence permits as well. Chip reading is a separate module from MRZ reading, and storing the document image is a data-protection decision rather than a hardware one.
How long does a custom enclosure take?
Typically three to six weeks from approved brief: one to two weeks for CAD and drawing approval, two to three weeks for a prototype, then three to four weeks for a production batch. The variables are the number of peripheral cut-outs and whether the finish is a standard colour or a brand match.
What steel thickness is normal for a kiosk cabinet?
1.2 to 2.0 mm cold-rolled steel for painted cabinets, commonly 1.5 mm on structural panels. Stainless is used where the front face is touched constantly and must not chip. Thicker sheet adds weight and cost without improving stiffness as much as a folded profile does.
Can we use our own colour and logo?
Yes. Colour is matched against a physical sample rather than a screen, and the logo can be printed, etched or fitted as a badge. Branding is approved on the prototype before the batch is coated, because changing colour mid-batch is not practical.
How much cheaper is a batch than a single prototype?
Fifty units typically come in 15 to 25% below a single-unit price, mostly through amortised programming, bending setup and coating batches. New tooling for a custom enclosure is a separate one-off cost that does not reduce with volume.
