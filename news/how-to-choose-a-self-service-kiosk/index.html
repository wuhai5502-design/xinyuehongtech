How to Choose a Self-Service Kiosk

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published October 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer: Start from the transaction and how long it takes, then fix screen size and mounting height, the module list with its interfaces, the operating system your software needs, and the environment. Compare quotes on the same module list, then pilot two to four units before volume.

Start from the transaction, not the catalogue

Write the transaction down as a sequence of steps and time each one. A wayfinding lookup is 15 seconds and never touches a printer. A restaurant order is 90 seconds, ends with two receipts and often a payment card. A hospital registration is two to three minutes, needs an ID document, a payment and a printed slip. Those three numbers — 15 seconds, 90 seconds, three minutes — set the screen size, the peripheral list and the cabinet size far more reliably than a product photo.

Note the error paths at the same time. What happens when the card is declined, the passport chip cannot be read, or the printer is empty? A kiosk that handles the happy path in 30 seconds and the failure path in five minutes will generate staff interventions, and staff interventions are the cost that kills the business case.

Match the screen to the task

Screen size follows the amount of information the customer has to read and the number of targets they have to hit. A directory or a single-question check-in works on 15.6 to 21.5 inches in landscape. A food menu with modifiers needs 21.5 to 27 inches, portrait, because the category rail and the basket both want horizontal space. A multi-service terminal — immigration, banking, hotel, retail in one cabinet — goes to 32 inches with a QHD panel so two content zones can coexist without shrinking the type.

Mounting height matters as much as size. Controls between 15 and 48 inches above the finished floor satisfy ADA 308, and a screen centre around 1,200 to 1,400 mm suits a standing adult. Portrait orientation puts more of the screen inside that band. If the unit also serves seated users, plan a 30 by 48 inch clear floor in front of it and keep the primary buttons in the lower half.

List the peripherals with their interfaces

Write the list as a table with four columns: module, model, interface, who supplies the driver. Most integration surprises come from that last column. A receipt printer may be USB with a vendor SDK, a serial device with a virtual COM port, or a network printer that needs a fixed IP. A passport reader may present itself as a keyboard wedge or require a full SDK. A payment terminal is its own certified device and connects over USB, serial or Ethernet depending on the provider.

Separate mandatory modules from optional ones before you ask for prices. A quote that lists every possible module is not comparable to one that lists the required set. Payment is the clearest example: the terminal, its certification and its integration are usually supplied through an acquirer, so the kiosk's job is to provide a mounting position, power and a data path — not to certify the payment application.

Decide the operating system from the software, not the hardware

Windows suits applications that depend on a full driver stack — serial devices, legacy POS clients, USB peripherals with Windows-only SDKs. Android suits content-led applications, digital signage and web apps, and boots faster, but the vendor's Android build and its peripheral support have to be confirmed module by module. Linux suits teams with in-house engineering and a fixed peripheral set.

The practical test is a list: for every module, does the chosen platform have a driver and a supported SDK? If two modules only exist on Windows, the decision is made for you. If the application is a browser page and the peripherals are USB HID, Android will usually cost less to run.

Check the deployment conditions

Indoor lobby and outdoor kerbside are different machines. Indoor units can use a standard front face; wet or dusty positions need a sealed front and a defined IP rating, and public unsupervised sites need an impact rating for the glass. Brightness is the other variable: 400 to 500 nits is fine under a ceiling, 700 to 1,000 nits is normal for a screen that faces daylight, and anti-glare glass matters more than the peak figure.

Also confirm the duty cycle. A terminal that runs 24/7 with no restart window needs industrial components and a thermal design that removes heat without a fan; a terminal that runs eight hours a day in a staffed lobby can be built closer to commercial hardware.

Compare quotations on the same basis

Ask for a quotation in the module structure you defined, with each line marked included, optional or excluded. Freight, duties, installation, software integration and the spare-parts kit are the five lines most often left out, and they are the five that change the total. A quote that states the certification scope per module is worth more than one that says "CE certified" without saying for what.

Pilot before volume

Run two to four units for four to six weeks and measure transactions per unit per day, average session time, intervention rate and the top three failure reasons. Those four numbers decide the roll-out mix better than any specification review. They also tell you whether the screen was the right size — if staff are regularly leaning in to press a button, it was not.

Common questions

What is the first thing to decide when specifying a kiosk?
The transaction the customer completes, written as steps and timed. A 15-second lookup and a three-minute registration with a document reader produce different screens, different modules and different cabinets. Starting from a product photo usually ends with a unit that is either oversized or missing a peripheral.
Should the kiosk run Windows, Android or Linux?
Follow the software and the peripheral drivers. If the application needs a full driver stack or vendor SDKs that only exist on Windows, use Windows. If it is a browser application with USB HID peripherals, Android usually costs less to run. Linux suits teams with engineering capacity and a fixed module list.
How do I compare two kiosk quotations fairly?
Fix the module list first, then ask each supplier to mark every line as included, optional or excluded, and to state the certification scope per module. Add freight, duties, installation, integration and spares before comparing totals; those five lines account for most of the difference between quotes.
