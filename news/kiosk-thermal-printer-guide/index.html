Choosing an 80 mm Thermal Printer for a Kiosk

By Mark, Project Engineer · Published November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

The short version: The receipt printer is the part of a kiosk that fails first, so it is specified before the enclosure is detailed. Four numbers decide the build: paper width (80 mm standard), the stock and cutter rating, whether the roll loads from the front or the rear, and whether a paper-near-end sensor is wired to the management software. Get those four right and the printer stops being the reason a terminal goes out of service.

Why the printer decides the cabinet

A printer is a moving mechanical part in a machine that is otherwise solid-state. The paper path, the cutter, the outlet and the restock door all occupy space, and they all have to be reachable by the person who reloads the roll. Decide the printer early, because changing it later usually means re-cutting the front panel, not swapping a module.

On a kiosk the printer serves two audiences at once: the customer reads the receipt, and the operator restocks the paper. The outlet height belongs to the customer; the loading access belongs to the operator. Most printer complaints trace back to one of the two being ignored.

Paper stock sets the mechanism

80 mm is the standard width, but width is the easy part. The stock decides whether the printer works: thermal paper needs the right coating and thickness for the mechanism, and a pre-printed or heavier roll changes the paper path and the cutter rating.

Specify the stock before the printer. A kiosk that runs out of the approved paper and gets loaded with a thicker roll from the stationery cupboard will jam, and the fault will be reported as the printer’s, not the paper’s.

Cutter, and what a jam costs

An auto-cutter is standard, but there are two kinds of cut: full guillotine and partial cut with a tear bar. The partial cut leaves the receipt hanging by a tab, which is quieter and cheaper, and the customer tears it. Where the receipt is a legal or tax document, a full cut and a consistent length matter more.

A jam is not the printer’s fault alone. Most jams are paper-related: a curl in the roll, a foreign object in the path, or a cutter that has not been cleaned. The answer is a visible tear bar, a front-loading design, and a jam message on screen that tells the customer the receipt did not print.

Front or rear loading, and the unattended night

Front-loading printers let a steward swap the roll in thirty seconds without opening the electronics bay. Rear-loading keeps the front face clean but forces the operator to open the cabinet, which is why front-loading wins on most public terminals.

The second design detail is the paper-near-end sensor. It warns the management system before the roll runs out, so a kiosk does not fail silently at 2 a.m. Wire it into the same dashboard that reports the card reader and the touchscreen.

Specification checklist

  • Paper width and stock: 80 mm thermal, with the exact thickness and coating approved for the mechanism.
  • Cutter type: full guillotine or partial cut, matched to whether the receipt is a tax document.
  • Loading direction: front-loading for unattended units, with the roll behind a locked door.
  • Paper-near-end sensor, wired into the management software.
  • Outlet height and angle, reachable by a standing adult without bending.
  • Duty cycle, matched to the peak transactions per hour rather than the daily average.

Printer questions

Which printer is right for a kiosk: receipt, ticket or A4?
Receipt, ticket and A4 printing use different machines with different mechanisms and outlets. An 80 mm thermal printer covers receipts and queue tickets. Event tickets with a pre-printed stock, or A4 documents such as medical reports, need a different mechanism and a different outlet, and are usually quoted as a separate cabinet variant rather than an option.
Why does the printer jam after a reload?
Almost always the stock: a heavier or pre-printed roll, a curl near the core, or the paper seated off-centre. A front-loading mechanism with a visible path reduces the cases where the operator is the cause, and a printed loading label beside the door removes most of the rest.
Should the receipt printer be customer-facing or hidden?
The outlet is customer-facing; the mechanism and the roll should be behind a locked door. Keep the tear bar at the outlet so the customer takes the receipt cleanly, and keep the paper path serviceable from the front.
What happens when the paper runs out at night?
The paper-near-end sensor warns before the roll empties, and the terminal shows a message when it does. On unattended units the fallback is a digital receipt on screen or by QR, agreed with the operator before deployment.
Does the printer affect kiosk certification?
The printer affects the electrical and EMC file of the assembled terminal, and its paper-path design affects ingress. The printer itself carries the vendor’s approval; the kiosk certificate covers the unit as built with that printer inside it.
