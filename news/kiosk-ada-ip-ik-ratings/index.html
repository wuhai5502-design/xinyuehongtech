Kiosk ADA Height, IP65 and IK Ratings Explained

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published November 10, 2026 · 9 min read

Quick answer: On a public self-service terminal the reach range is 15 to 48 inches above finished floor (ADA 308/309), the usual ingress protection is IP54 indoors and IP65 where the unit is washed or rained on, and public glass is normally specified at IK08 with IK10 for unsupervised sites. A 30 by 48 inch clear floor space is required in front of the unit.

Three numbers decide most of the mechanical design of a public terminal: the reach range the users have to work within, the ingress protection the site demands, and the impact energy the front glass has to survive. Get them wrong and the cabinet is rebuilt; get them right at drawing stage and they cost almost nothing.

The three constraints at a glance

Reference values. Local codes and the client's own accessibility policy always take precedence.

Reach rangeADA 2010, sections 308 and 309 (US); EN 301 549 and ISO 21542 elsewhereOperable parts between 15 in and 48 in above finished floor

Ingress protectionIEC 60529IP65 for wash-down and exposed sites; IP54 for covered corridors

ADA: what the standard actually asks of a kiosk

The Americans with Disabilities Act is a US civil-rights law, and its 2010 Standards for Accessible Design set dimensional rules that apply to self-service equipment in places of public accommodation. The two sections that matter most for a kiosk are 308, which defines reach ranges, and 309, which defines how an operable part must work.

In practice this produces four requirements on the drawing:

  • Height. Every control the customer must touch — screen, card reader, PIN pad, scanner, ticket outlet — has to sit between 15 inches and 48 inches above the finished floor. On a floor-standing kiosk that usually means the interactive zone is centred around 40 to 44 inches, not at the 60-inch height that looks good in a render.
  • Reach depth. A forward reach to 48 inches is only allowed over an obstruction no deeper than 20 inches. Put the card reader 25 inches back on a counter and the allowed height drops to 44 inches. Deep counters are where accessible designs most often fail.
  • Clear floor space. A 30 by 48 inch clear area is needed in front of the unit, positioned for either a forward or a parallel approach. This is a floor-planning item, not a cabinet item, but it is decided at the same meeting.
  • Operation. Controls must work with one hand, without tight grasping, pinching or twisting, and with an operating force under five pounds. A recessed button that needs a fingernail, or a card slot with a stiff flap, fails this test regardless of where it is mounted.

Two practical consequences. First, if the same terminal serves standing adults and wheelchair users, either the whole interactive zone is lowered and the screen tilted, or a second lower screen is added — the mobile hospital station and the dual-screen government terminal are two answers to that problem. Second, a kiosk designed to ADA reach ranges is usually also comfortable for elderly users and for anyone carrying a bag, which is why the constraint is worth applying even in markets where the law does not require it.

IP ratings: the two digits, decoded

IP codes come from IEC 60529. The first digit is solids, the second is water, and a higher number always includes the lower ones. Only the first digit goes to 6; the second can go to 9K.

Ingress protection levels that appear in kiosk specifications.

RatingSolidsWaterWhere it is appropriate
IP54Dust protectedSplashing waterCovered corridors, hospital wards, office lobbies
IP65Dust tightWater jets from a nozzleWash-down areas, covered outdoor bays, food production
IP66Dust tightPowerful water jetsExposed kerbside units, vehicle wash-down zones

The rating applies to the enclosure as tested, not to the whole machine in service. A unit rated IP65 with the service door open is not IP65, and a cable gland left loose at installation cancels the rating just as effectively. That is why the specification should say which surfaces carry the rating — front face only, or the complete cabinet — and how the cable entry is sealed.

For an indoor terminal in a dry hall, IP54 is enough and cheaper. For anything that will be hosed down or rained on, the front face needs a sealed bezel and the rear needs a gasketed door; the parking payment terminal is built around that logic.

IK ratings: impact, not water

IK codes come from IEC 62262 and describe how much mechanical impact the enclosure survives, expressed in joules. IK08 is 5 joules, roughly a 1.7 kg mass dropped from 30 cm; IK10 is 20 joules, roughly a 5 kg mass from 40 cm.

On a kiosk the rating is really a statement about two different things: the cover glass, and the cabinet shell. A 3 mm chemically strengthened glass can pass IK08 while a 6 mm annealed glass of the same size may not, because the failure is about how the glass breaks, not how thick it is. The shell matters for the corners and the service door, which is where a trolley or a delivery cart usually lands.

For unsupervised sites — a station forecourt, a 24-hour lobby, a car park — IK10 on the front glass and a bolted plinth are the normal combination. In a staffed hospital corridor, IK08 is usually sufficient and the money is better spent on the touch stack.

Brightness and touch in daylight

Brightness is measured in nits (cd/m²). A standard indoor panel runs 250 to 350 nits; a window-facing lobby needs 500 to 700; direct sunlight needs 1,000 or more, plus an anti-reflective treatment, or the screen becomes a mirror.

Typical brightness targets by position.

PositionBrightnessNote
Indoor hall, no daylight250 – 350 nitsStandard panel, lowest cost
Lobby or corridor with windows500 – 700 nitsAnti-glare glass, auto brightness
Covered outdoor bay1,000 nitsLight sensor, thermal management
Direct sun1,500 – 2,500 nitsHeated or cooled enclosure, IR touch often preferred

Touch technology interacts with the environment. Projected capacitive (PCAP) gives the best image and multi-touch, but a thick glove or a film of water breaks contact. Infrared touch works through gloves and scratches less visibly, at the cost of a deeper bezel and sensitivity to dust on the frame. Resistive still has a place where the operator wears heavy gloves and uses a stylus.

Heat is the other half of the outdoor problem. A sealed enclosure in direct sun can sit 20 to 30 °C above ambient inside, which is why outdoor units are usually specified with an air-conditioned or heat-exchanger cabinet rather than a fan, and why the panel's operating temperature range is quoted separately from the enclosure's.

How these constraints reach the sheet metal

None of the above is a software problem, and all of it lands on the drawing:

  • The interactive zone is set by the reach range, which sets the cabinet height and the screen tilt — not the other way round.
  • IP rating sets the door gasket, the cable gland, the seam welding and the powder-coat coverage on cut edges.
  • IK rating sets the glass specification, the bezel retention and the plinth fixings.
  • Brightness and heat set the panel, the glass coating and whether the cabinet carries a heat exchanger.
  • All of it changes the maintenance access, which is why the service door is usually the last thing drawn and the first thing to regret.

If the project is a standard cabinet, these are options and the quotation lists them. If the project is a new enclosure, they are inputs to the CAD model, and changing them after the first prototype is what turns a four-week build into a twelve-week one.

Questions engineers ask first

Does a self-service kiosk have to be ADA compliant?
In the United States, self-service equipment in places of public accommodation is covered by the ADA, and the 2010 Standards set reach and operable-part requirements that apply to it. Other markets have their own rules, commonly EN 301 549 or a national building code. Local counsel or the authority having jurisdiction decides applicability; the dimensional guidance is the same.
What height should a kiosk screen be?
For a standing adult, the screen centre usually sits between 40 and 44 inches above finished floor, so that the whole interactive zone falls inside the 15 to 48 inch reach range. If the terminal also serves wheelchair users without a second screen, the zone is lowered and the screen tilted rather than left at standing height.
Does IP65 matter for an indoor kiosk?
Usually not. A dry indoor hall is adequately served by IP54, and paying for IP65 adds cost in gaskets, sealed connectors and finishing. IP65 or better is specified where the unit is hosed down, placed in a covered outdoor bay, or installed in a food-production area.
Is IK10 better than IK08 for a kiosk?
IK10 survives four times the impact energy of IK08, and it is the right choice for unsupervised sites where the front glass is exposed to trolleys, carts or deliberate damage. In a staffed indoor corridor, IK08 is usually sufficient and the budget is better spent on the touch stack or the printer.
Can projected capacitive touch work outdoors?
PCAP works outdoors but is sensitive to water films and thick gloves, both of which are common outdoors. Infrared touch is often the better choice for exposed sites because it reads through gloves and a wet screen, at the cost of a deeper bezel and more attention to dust on the frame.
How bright does an outdoor kiosk screen need to be?
As a working guide: 500 to 700 nits for a lobby with windows, around 1,000 nits for a covered outdoor bay, and 1,500 nits or more with an anti-reflective treatment for direct sun. Brightness alone is not enough — a sealed cabinet in direct sun also needs thermal management, not just a brighter panel.
