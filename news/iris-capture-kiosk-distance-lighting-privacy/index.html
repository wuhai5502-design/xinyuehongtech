Iris Capture on a Self-Service Kiosk

By Mark, Project Engineer · Published November 20, 2026 · 8 min read

The short version: Iris recognition needs a defined capture zone, not just a camera. An embedded module normally expects the user at 30 to 60 cm, a long-range camera expects 1 m or more, and both need controlled light and a stable head position. If the kiosk stands in a glazed lobby, the lighting problem is solved before the hardware is chosen, not after.

Working distance sets the whole faceplate

An iris module has a distance window in which the eye is in focus and the illumination reaches the iris. Below it the image is blurred; above it the eye is too small in the frame. That window decides how far the user stands from the cabinet, which decides the floor space in front of it, the height of the camera and the angle of the faceplate.

A 30 to 40 cm module suits a terminal where the user leans in, like a border gate or a benefits enrolment desk. A 1 m camera suits walk-through identification, where the person keeps moving. Mixing the two intentions produces a kiosk that is comfortable for neither.

Light is the failure mode

Iris matching degrades faster than fingerprint under poor light. Direct sunlight through a glazed façade, a spotlight above the unit, and the reflection of the screen on a pair of glasses are the three common causes of retry loops. Ask for the module’s tolerance to ambient light, then measure the lux where the camera will point at the time of day the queue forms.

Near-infrared illumination helps and is standard on most modules, but it brings its own questions: does the module require a dark shroud, and does the IR visibly glow in a dark room?

Head position and the guidance UI

Iris capture wants the eye centred and still for a moment. The kiosk cannot force a chin rest on a public terminal, so the guidance has to do the work: a live preview, a distance indicator and a clear “move closer” message. On a floor-standing unit, the camera belongs near eye height for a standing adult, which conflicts with wheelchair reach; that conflict is settled on the drawing, not in software.

Biometric data is a legal question before a technical one

Iris templates are biometric data. In the EU they fall under the special categories in GDPR Article 9 and need a lawful basis and a retention rule; some jurisdictions, including several US states, require written consent before enrolment. Two technical choices follow: store a template rather than an image, and delete the template when the enrolment ends.

This is also where a kiosk supplier’s scope ends. The vendor certifies the reader, the operator owns the data and the legal basis, and the integration has to make that division visible to the user.

When iris is the wrong choice

Iris is a poor fit for children, for outdoor units with uncontrolled light, and for high-throughput queues where a two-second hold is too long. It is a strong fit where the population is enrolled in advance, the environment is controlled, and the identity check has to be difficult to spoof.

Capture distance and what it changes in the cabinet.

Preparing the request

The module and its stated distance range, the light level where the unit will stand, the population being enrolled and the retention rule for the templates. With those four answers the faceplate geometry and the guidance flow can be designed together.

What buyers ask

Can an iris module be added to an existing kiosk cabinet?
Sometimes. The limit is usually the faceplate: the camera needs a clear line at the right height and angle, and the panel behind it needs room for the module. A cabinet designed for a card reader rarely has that depth.
Does iris work through glasses and contact lenses?
Usually, if the user removes tinted or strongly reflective glasses. Sunglasses, wet lenses and heavy glare are the common failures. The guidance UI should ask the user to remove sunglasses rather than let the reader retry silently.
Is an iris image stored on the kiosk?
An iris image should not be stored on the kiosk. The standard pattern is to capture, extract a template and send that; the image is discarded. Storing images on a public terminal creates a data-protection problem with no operational benefit.
Can one kiosk support fingerprint and iris?
Yes, and it is common in enrolment projects. Each modality needs its own aperture and its own guidance, so the faceplate grows and the flow has to decide which credential the user presents first.
What certification applies to an iris reader?
The vendor certifies the reader, and the template format normally follows ISO/IEC 19794-6. The assembled terminal still needs its own electrical and EMC compliance, and the data handling is governed by the operator’s privacy basis.
