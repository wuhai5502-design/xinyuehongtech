How Much Does a Self-Service Kiosk Cost?

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published November 8, 2026 · 9 min read

Quick answer: A basic countertop self-service kiosk costs US$380–700 FOB Shenzhen. A 27–32-inch floor-standing kiosk with card readers, scanner and receipt printer runs US$1,300–2,600. Dual-screen public-service terminals with multiple document readers are US$1,800–3,400, and ATM-style units with a cash acceptor start around US$3,500. These are reference ranges for 2026; the final price is set by the module list, not by the cabinet.

Kiosk prices are quoted per build, not per category. The same 27-inch cabinet can leave the factory at US$900 or US$2,400 depending on which readers, printer, panel and computing platform are inside it. The ranges below are reference points for 2026, FOB Shenzhen, for orders from one unit to about 100 units.

Two assumptions sit behind those numbers: standard module set (no new tooling) and payment by bank transfer. Freight, import duty, market certification and software are quoted separately, because none of them belong to the cabinet.

What is inside the price

A quotation is a bill of materials with margin on top. In a typical kiosk build the split looks like this. Percentages are shares of the unit price, not of the cost.

The five things that move the price most

1. Screen size, panel grade and brightness

A 15.6-inch industrial panel and a 32-inch 2560 × 1440 panel are not the same purchase. Moving from a 21.5-inch 1080p panel to a 32-inch QHD panel typically adds US$250–500 to the bill of materials before any mechanical changes. High-brightness panels for outdoor or window-facing units add more again.

2. Touch technology

Projected capacitive (PCAP) is the default for indoor public terminals. Infrared touch is cheaper on large sizes but adds bezel depth and is more sensitive to dust. Resistive survives gloves and wet hands but feels dated. The gap between the cheapest and the most durable stack on the same screen is usually US$60–200.

3. Card readers, PIN pads and payment devices

This is where quotations diverge most. A contactless-only reader is inexpensive; a full set of chip, swipe and contactless plus a certified encrypting PIN pad can add several hundred dollars. Where the acquirer requires a specific certified payment terminal, that device is usually bought separately and mounted on the kiosk rather than integrated into it.

4. The computing platform

An Android board, an Intel Celeron fanless box and a Core i5 box with 16 GB of RAM sit in different price bands. The right question is not which is cheapest but which one your software, drivers and payment SDK actually run on. Replacing a platform after the enclosure is drawn costs far more than choosing the correct one at the start.

5. Order size, tooling and lead time

At one unit you pay for setup. At 100 units the same cabinet may drop 15–25%, mostly because laser programming, fixtures and powder-coat batches are amortised. New tooling for a custom enclosure is a one-off cost, typically US$2,000–8,000 depending on complexity, and it does not disappear at higher volumes. Rush orders add cost at the component level, not the assembly level.

Costs that are not on the quotation

Buyers comparing two kiosk quotes often compare the wrong totals. These lines are usually missing from both:

  • Market certification. CE, FCC or RoHS testing for a new model family typically runs US$1,500–6,000, once, and is not repeated per unit.
  • Payment approval. Acquirer and scheme fees for the payment device, paid to the provider rather than to the kiosk factory.
  • Spare parts and consumables. Budget 5–10% of the hardware value per year for printers, paper, readers and a small stock of spares.
  • Freight and insurance. A floor-standing kiosk is bulky freight, not parcel freight. Sea LCL is normal for pilots; air is used for samples and costs several times more.
  • Import duty and VAT. Depends entirely on the destination market and the HS code applied to the unit.
  • Software integration. Usually the largest non-hardware line in the project, and the one most often underestimated.
  • Installation and commissioning. Site preparation, power, network, anchoring and staff training.
  • Warranty logistics. Who pays the return freight for a failed unit, and how long a replacement takes.

How to compare two quotations

Ask both suppliers for the same eleven lines. If a quote cannot answer them, it is not comparable, however low the total looks.

The cheapest way to start

Most first projects do not need a new enclosure. They need a working terminal in front of real users, fast. A practical low-cost route:

  • Order one prototype unit from a standard cabinet and standard module set — minimum order quantity of one is common in this industry.
  • Skip custom tooling in round one. Prove the flow first; tool the enclosure once the service is validated.
  • Use a certified third-party payment terminal instead of an integrated payment module. It is cheaper to certify and easier to swap.
  • Run the pilot for four to six weeks and measure transactions per unit per day, not satisfaction scores.
  • Scale with a production batch that shares the pilot's enclosure, so the drawings, fixtures and test procedure are reused.

Terms used in kiosk quotations

MOQ
Minimum order quantity. For kiosk hardware this is often one unit for a standard build, and 20–50 units for a custom enclosure run.
FOB
Free on board. The price covers the goods loaded at the named port; freight, insurance and import duty are added by the buyer.
EPP
Encrypting PIN pad. A separately certified device for PIN entry; it is never replaced by the touchscreen.
PCAP
Projected capacitive touch. The technology behind most modern kiosk and phone screens; supports multi-touch and a flush glass surface.

Questions buyers ask most

How much does a basic self-service kiosk cost?
A countertop unit with a 15.6-inch screen and an Android or Celeron platform starts around US$380–700 FOB Shenzhen. Adding a card reader and a receipt printer usually moves the same build into the US$700–1,100 range.
Why do two quotes for the same kiosk differ by a factor of two?
Almost always the components behind the cabinet: panel brand and brightness, touch technology, reader models, printer duty cycle, and whether the computer is an Android board or an Intel box. A quote that lists only the enclosure cannot be compared with one that lists the bill of materials.
Does ordering more units reduce the price?
Yes, typically 15–25% at 100 units against a single-unit price, because programming, fixtures, powder-coat batches and test setup are amortised. Tooling for a new enclosure is a separate one-off cost that does not scale down this way.
Is the payment terminal included in the kiosk price?
Usually not. Where the acquirer requires a certified device, the kiosk is quoted with the mounting, power and cable route, and the payment terminal is bought through the provider. Some projects integrate a reader instead, but that changes the certification path.
How much does shipping a kiosk cost?
It depends on volume and route, not on the unit price. A floor-standing kiosk is bulky freight: sea LCL is normal for pilot quantities, a 40-foot container is normal for a roll-out. Air freight is used for samples and typically costs several times the sea rate.
Is CE or FCC certification included?
It is quoted as a separate line because testing is per model family, not per unit. Budget US$1,500–6,000 for a new family, once. If a supplier says certification is included, ask which laboratory and which report number.
Does the price include software?
No. Kiosk hardware is quoted with the operating system image and driver support, but the service application is written by you or your integrator, and it is usually the largest non-hardware cost in the project.
What lead time should I expect?
Roughly 15 days from deposit and approved drawing for a standard build inside the standard module set. A new enclosure adds drawing time and a prototype cycle; a production batch with new tooling typically takes four to eight weeks.
Can I buy a single unit as a sample?
Yes. Minimum order quantity of one is normal for standard cabinets, and the sample should go through the same burn-in and inspection route as a production batch, so it represents what the batch will be.
