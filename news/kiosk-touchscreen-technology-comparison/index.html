Kiosk Touchscreen Technologies Compared

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published November 4, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer: PCAP, infrared and SAW touchscreens behave differently behind glass, in rain, with gloves and after vandalism. A practical comparison for kiosk buyers.

Most touchscreen comparisons are written for phones and tablets. A self-service kiosk is a different machine: the screen sits behind glass or a bezel, strangers press it all day, cleaning crews spray it, and the enclosure lives outdoors or in a dusty hall. Under those conditions the "best" technology on a spec sheet is often not the best technology behind sheet metal.

This article compares the three touch technologies installed most often — projected capacitive (PCAP), infrared (IR) and surface acoustic wave (SAW) — from the installer's and the operator's point of view.

How each technology actually senses a touch

Projected capacitive (PCAP) measures the disturbance of a grid of tiny electrical fields. The sensor is a glass or film laminate, and the controller continuously scans rows and columns. Anything conductive — a finger, a thin glove, a stylus — changes the local capacitance and is reported as a touch point.

Infrared (IR) places a frame of LED emitters and receivers around the perimeter of the display. A finger breaks a set of invisible light beams crossing the surface, and the controller triangulates the position from the interrupted beams. The touch surface is simply whatever glass or plastic you put over the frame.

Surface acoustic wave (SAW) sends ultrasonic waves across the surface of the glass itself. A finger absorbs part of the wave at the touch point, and the controller calculates the coordinates from the attenuation pattern.

The sensing method explains almost every practical difference below.

Durability against the public

PCAP is the most forgiving against vandalism because the sensor sits behind the cover glass; scratching the surface degrades optics, not touch function. Multi-touch gestures, edge swipes and signature capture all work naturally.

IR frames are exposed at the bezel. They survive hard use well — there is nothing on the glass to wear out — but the optical strip is the vulnerable part: dust, condensation, paint splatter or a jammed flyer can block beams and produce phantom touches. Quality varies a lot between frame suppliers, mainly in how well the firmware filters interference from sunlight and compact-fluorescent lighting.

SAW glass has no overlays and keeps excellent optical clarity, but the wave needs a bare, slightly damp fingertip to absorb energy properly. Hard styluses, thick gloves and dirty screens all degrade accuracy. For public units that get abused, SAW is usually the third choice today.

Rain, dust, gloves and cleaning chemicals

This is where the technologies separate most clearly:

For outdoor parking, ticketing and border lanes, the default recommendation is PCAP behind chemically strengthened cover glass, with the controller tuned for water rejection. For indoor units in very dusty industrial halls, IR frames with sealed bevels are a proven alternative when multi-touch is not required.

Optics and enclosure integration

PCAP laminates add layers to the stack, so anti-reflective treatment matters more: without AR coating, sunlight can wash the display out and the controller picks up interference. Budget for the AR or anti-glare cover glass when specifying outdoor PCAP — it is not an option, it is part of the touch system.

IR adds a visible bezel around the display, typically 15–20 mm per side on larger panels. That is why flush-glass designs with narrow borders almost always use PCAP: the IR frame physically does not fit behind 3 mm borders.

SAW offers the cleanest optics of the three — no layers, no bezel — which is why it still appears on indoor directory and information kiosks where hands are clean and vandalism is rare.

What to recommend, in practice

  • Outdoor or semi-outdoor units (parking, ticketing, EV charging, border): PCAP with strengthened cover glass, AR coating and water-rejection controller tuning. Single-touch is acceptable if the application does not need gestures.
  • Indoor public units (queuing, registration, directories): PCAP as the default; IR where budget is tight, multi-touch is unnecessary, and dust can be controlled.
  • Clerk-operated counters and back-office terminals: any of the three works; PCAP still wins on handwriting and signature quality.
  • Any unit handling PIN entry: remember the touch layer is separate from the payment terminal — the encrypting PIN pad is its own certified device and is never replaced by a touchscreen.
