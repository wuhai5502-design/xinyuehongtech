The CRA Reporting Clock Started in September. Kiosk Builds Are Still Catching Up

By Xinyuehong Editorial · Published November 13, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer: Since 11 September 2026 the EU Cyber Resilience Act requires manufacturers of products with digital elements to report actively exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours, then 72 hours, then 14 days after a fix. A self-service kiosk counts. Most kiosk projects cannot yet say which software build sits in which unit, which is exactly the information the first deadline asks for.

What counts as a product with digital elements

The cabinet is not the product. The unit that goes into a hospital lobby or a bank branch is a computer running Windows, Android or Linux, an application, and a set of peripheral modules, sold as one thing. If it has any data connection, and almost every terminal does, it falls inside the definition in the Cyber Resilience Act.

Article 3(13) puts the obligation on whoever markets the product under its own name or trademark. That is usually the company whose logo is on the front panel, not the sheet-metal shop that cut the enclosure and not the board vendor. An importer or distributor that rebrands a terminal, or modifies it substantially, takes on manufacturer duties as well. For a Chinese-built kiosk sold into the EU, the practical question is which company is legally on the hook, and it should be answered in the contract rather than worked out after an incident.

The clock: 24 hours, 72 hours, 14 days

Reporting opened on 11 September 2026 and runs through the ENISA single reporting platform, which forwards a single notification to the CSIRT of the member state coordinating the case. The deadlines are short enough that the process has to exist before it is needed.

Article 14 reporting stages

StageDeadlineWhat it contains
Early warning24 hours after becoming awareThat the vulnerability is actively exploited, the affected product, and whether malicious actors are suspected.
Notification72 hoursProduct and version, nature of the exploit, corrective or mitigating measures already available to users.
Final report14 days after a fix existsSeverity and impact, any known actor, and the details of the update or workaround.
Severe incident24 h / 72 h / 1 monthSame early warning and notification pattern, with the final report one month after the notification.

The trigger is the moment you have reasonable certainty, not the moment you have a fix. That is the part manufacturers underestimate. A support engineer reading a customer report on a Monday morning can start a 24-hour clock, and the first notification does not need a root cause, only a statement that exploitation is happening.

Jurisdiction follows the member state where cybersecurity decisions about the product are mainly taken. A manufacturer with no EU establishment gets routed by reference to its authorised representative, importer or distributor, which is one more reason to know who that is before a firmware team in Shenzhen has to file anything in Europe.

Where kiosk builds actually fall over

The Windows image nobody owns. Plenty of kiosk fleets ship with a captured image: drivers baked in, kiosk-mode shell pre-configured, no patch process. When a vulnerability lands in a printer driver or in the shell, the supplier cannot say which of the 400 units in the field run the affected build. A component list per shipment, with versions, is unglamorous work. It is also the difference between a 24-hour answer and a week of guessing.

The module with no maintainer. A receipt printer SDK last updated in 2019. A card reader vendor acquired twice. An Android BSP for a board that has been superseded. None of these are unusual in this industry. Under Article 14 you only report when the vulnerability is exploitable in your product, but you cannot assess exploitability if the module vendor publishes nothing. Ask for the security contact and the advisory feed when you sign the purchase order, not when a CVE appears.

The support period promise. The CRA expects a declared support period, during which vulnerabilities are handled. Kiosks stay in service for seven to ten years. Promise five years of security updates and you have committed to spare PCs, operating-system licensing, a re-imaging process and a test rig that can validate a patch against every module in the cabinet. That is a budget line, and it belongs in the quotation rather than in the small print afterwards.

What the September date does not require

It does not require CE marking. The essential requirements, the technical documentation and the conformity assessment apply from 11 December 2027. It does not require reporting every vulnerability, only actively exploited ones. And it does not require you to report a flaw that exists in a component but cannot be exploited in your configuration.

What it does require is knowing your own build. In practice that means four things: a component inventory per product version, a fixed update path for units in the field, a named security contact reachable outside office hours, and a written support period that sales is not allowed to improvise.

Six questions to put in the next RFQ

Buyers in Europe have started asking these. Suppliers who cannot answer lose the shortlist.

  • Which operating system image and version ships, and who is responsible for patching it after delivery?
  • Can you provide a component list with versions for each shipment, not just for the reference build?
  • What support period do you declare in writing, and what happens when it ends?
  • Which third-party modules have a public vulnerability disclosure channel, and where is it?
  • Who is the security contact, and what is the escalation path when a report arrives at 2 a.m.?
  • When a fix is released, how do the units already installed receive it?

None of these are difficult questions. The difficulty is that they cut across departments: sales promises a support period, engineering owns the image, procurement owns the module vendors, and legal signs the contract. A kiosk project that has not assigned those four responsibilities will answer the first question confidently and the fifth one badly.

The unglamorous part

A kiosk fleet is an IT estate of a few hundred devices, each with a screen, a computer, a printer and a reader, distributed across branches and lobbies. The CRA asks manufacturers to run it like one. That is not a certification exercise you can buy at the end of a project. It is a documentation habit that starts with the first prototype and continues for as long as the last unit is in the field.

For anyone specifying terminals in Europe this year, the practical filter is simple: ask the six questions, and see which supplier answers all of them without forwarding your email to someone else.

Common questions

Does the CRA apply to kiosks that were installed before September 2026?
Yes. The Article 14 reporting duty covers products already on the market, not only new ones shipped after 11 September 2026. If an actively exploited vulnerability in a terminal you supplied becomes known after that date, the clock starts regardless of when the unit was built. Knowing which serial numbers run which software build is therefore the first practical requirement.
Do we have to report every vulnerability we find?
No. The obligation covers vulnerabilities with reliable evidence of active exploitation, and severe incidents affecting your own development or update processes. Findings from internal testing or security research can be reported voluntarily through the same platform. A vulnerability that exists in a third-party component but is not exploitable in your configuration does not trigger a report, though it still has to be fixed upstream.
When does CE marking under the CRA become mandatory?
The substantive obligations, including security-by-design requirements, vulnerability handling, technical documentation and conformity assessment, apply from 11 December 2027. The September 2026 date only covers reporting. A kiosk delivered between the two dates is not yet CE-marked under the CRA, but it is already inside the reporting regime.
