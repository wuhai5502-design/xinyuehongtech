Airport Self Check-in Kiosks & Bag Tag Printing

A departures hall is a throughput problem measured in seconds per passenger. The constraint is rarely the screen; it is the document check, the boarding pass, and the bag tag — three different printers and readers in one footprint, working under a queue that forms three hours before departure.

Where the departures queue actually forms

Check-in opens two to three hours before departure and peaks in a 40-minute window. A single desk handles roughly one passenger every 90 seconds when documents are in order; the queue grows when a passport needs a second look or a bag exceeds the allowance. A self-service unit earns its place by taking the passengers whose documents are clean and whose bag can be self-tagged.

The second pressure point is the transfer passenger. They arrive with a boarding pass already issued, need no bag tag, and only want a seat change or a reprint. Sending them to the same queue as a full check-in wastes the desk's capacity; a kiosk that can reprint and re-seat absorbs them in under a minute.

The third is language. An international airport may need six or eight languages on the same screen, and the document prompts have to be unambiguous in all of them. That is a layout decision — the longest translated string sets the button width — not a translation exercise added at the end.

What an airport specification has to settle

DOCUMENTS

Passport and visa capture

MRZ reading is the baseline; chip reading and visa pages depend on the reader module and on what the airline's DCS accepts. The reader set is fixed before the deck is drawn.

BOARDING PASS

ATB or A4

ATB stock needs a dedicated printer and a defined paper path; A4 is cheaper but slower per passenger. Both can be fitted, and the choice follows the airline's boarding process.

BAG TAG

IATA tag stock

Bag tag printing needs a printer module sized for the tag stock and a collection point at standing height. The printer, not the screen, is usually the throughput limit.

SOFTWARE

CUSS and DCS

Airport common-use deployments normally require a CUSS-certified application approved by the airport authority. The kiosk supplies the peripheral layer; the certification lives with the application provider.

THROUGHPUT

Seconds per passenger

Document read, seat confirmation, two print jobs. If the bag tag and boarding pass come out of the same slot, the queue stalls; two outlets keep it moving.

UPTIME

Restock and jams

Tag stock, boarding pass stock and receipt paper are three consumables with three access points. All of them have to be replaceable by airport staff during a turnaround.

Procurement checklist

  • The DCS or check-in platform, and whether a CUSS-certified application is mandatory at the airport
  • Document types accepted: passport MRZ, chip, national ID, visa
  • Boarding pass format (ATB stock or A4) and bag tag stock specification
  • Bag tag and boarding pass outlet positions, and whether one slot or two
  • Languages required, and the longest translated string
  • Hall layout: power, network, floor fixing, and the queue geometry around the unit
  • Consumable restock procedure and who performs it
  • Pilot scope: one terminal bank, measured on passengers processed per hour and intervention rate

Airport projects move fastest when the application provider is already CUSS-certified and the peripheral list is fixed. We can supply reference details of self check-in units delivered for airport projects on request.

Questions we are asked first

Can the kiosk print bag tags?
Yes. A bag tag printer module can be fitted alongside the boarding pass printer, with the tag outlet at standing height. The tag stock specification and the printer model are confirmed with the airline's process before the cabinet is drawn, because the printer — not the screen — usually sets the throughput.
Does the kiosk support CUSS?
Common-use self-service at airports normally requires a CUSS-certified application approved by the airport authority. That certification belongs to the application provider; the kiosk supplies the document reader, printers and the peripheral interface the application calls.
Can it print both a boarding pass and a bag tag?
Yes, and it is better to have two outlets rather than one shared slot. A single slot forces the passenger to wait for two jobs to finish at the same point, which is where the queue stalls at peak.
Which documents can it read?
Passport MRZ reading as standard, chip reading depending on the reader module, plus national ID and visa pages where the reader set supports them. Send the document list the airline accepts and the readers are specified against it.
How many passengers per hour can one unit handle?
The practical limit is the print jobs, not the screen. A unit issuing one boarding pass runs faster than one issuing a boarding pass and a bag tag; the exact figure is measured on the pilot, and it is the number to design the terminal bank around.
Can the interface run in several languages?
Yes. The screen is sized against the longest translated string, and right-to-left languages such as Arabic are handled as a layout change rather than a translation. Language switching is implemented in the application.
