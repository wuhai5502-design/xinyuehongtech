Hospital Self-Service Kiosks

An outpatient visit is not one transaction. It is registration, a queue number, a consultation fee, a test payment and a report collection, each at a different counter. A hospital kiosk project only works when the hardware is mapped to those steps first.

Where the outpatient queue actually breaks

In most hospitals the first bottleneck is not the doctor, it is the registration window at 08:00. A patient who arrives without an appointment joins a queue to be registered, then a second queue to pay, then a third to collect a report. Each counter repeats the same identity check, and each handover is where the wait accumulates.

The second bottleneck appears after the consultation: the payment step. A patient who has just been told what tests they need is sent back to a cashier desk, which is often the busiest window in the building. Moving that payment next to the consultation area — or onto the ward — removes a queue without adding staff.

The third is the report. Radiology and laboratory results are printed at a counter that closes earlier than the wards, so a patient who finishes tests in the afternoon comes back the next morning for a piece of paper. A printing terminal that verifies identity and releases the document on demand removes that second visit entirely.

These three steps have different hardware requirements, which is why a single "hospital kiosk" rarely fits all of them. The guide below maps each step to a cabinet, and the cabinet to the modules that make it work.

What a hospital specification has to include

Six items that cause most rework when they are decided late.

IDENTITY

Three identity routes

ID card, medical insurance card and a booking reference are three different reads. Decide which the registration flow accepts before the reader deck is drawn, not after the cabinet is built.

PAYMENT

Payment inside or beside

A certified third-party terminal is the usual route, mounted on the cabinet with its own approval. An integrated reader is possible where the acquirer and the hospital's finance team both accept it.

PRINTING

Slip or A4

Queue tickets and receipts need an 80 mm thermal printer. Records, invoices and itemised bills need A4, which changes the cabinet and the paper access — usually to a front-loading design.

CLEANING

Wipeable front face

Flat glass, stainless keycaps and a retractable keyboard are specified because the front face is cleaned between patients. Deep recesses and fabric surfaces are the parts infection-control teams reject.

ACCESS

Height and reach

Seated patients, wheelchair users and elderly users set the screen height, the keyboard reach and whether a second lower screen is needed. This is fixed at the drawing stage with the hospital's accessibility standard The ADA reach-range guide sets out the numbers.

NETWORK

One cable, one policy

The kiosk needs a stable wired connection to the hospital system, and a maintenance window that does not clash with clinic hours. Remote update policy belongs in the project plan, not in the hardware order.

Procurement checklist

What to have on paper before you ask for a quotation.

  • The registration flow, written as steps, including which identity document is read at which step
  • Which payments the terminal must take, and whether a certified third-party device is mandatory
  • What is printed at each terminal: queue slip, receipt, invoice or full record
  • Where each unit stands: outpatient hall, corridor, ward, lift lobby, or a mobile station
  • Who uses it: standing adults only, or also seated and wheelchair users
  • The hospital system it must talk to, and the interface that system exposes
  • Cleaning policy and the surfaces that policy excludes
  • Pilot scope: how many units, in which department, for how long before the roll-out decision

Projects that arrive with this list usually move from enquiry to drawing in a week. Projects that arrive with only a photo of a machine take longer, because every one of these points has to be discovered by asking.

Questions we are asked first

Can a hospital kiosk integrate with our existing HIS or booking system?
The kiosk exposes its readers, printer and payment device to your application through standard interfaces; the hospital system integration is done on your side or by your software partner. What we need at specification stage is the interface that system offers — a local agent, a web session or an API — because that decides the platform.
Do we need one kiosk model for registration and another for payment?
Not necessarily. The dual-screen registration and payment terminal covers both steps in one cabinet, which is the usual choice for an outpatient hall. A separate payment-only unit makes sense where payment happens elsewhere: a ward corridor, a lift lobby or a discharge desk.
How is patient data handled on the terminal?
The kiosk reads the document or card and passes the result to your application; it does not keep a local copy of the record. Whether an image is stored, and for how long, is a policy decision implemented in the hospital system, not in the terminal firmware.
What is a realistic pilot before a full roll-out?
A common pattern is two to four units in one outpatient hall for four to six weeks, measured on transactions per unit per day and on how often staff have to intervene. That data decides the roll-out mix far better than a department-by-department guess.
