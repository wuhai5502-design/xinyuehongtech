Bank & Branch Self-Service Kiosks

Branch self-service is a privacy problem before it is a hardware problem. Every design decision — screen recess, PIN pad placement, queue direction, cabinet depth — follows from the fact that a customer is entering a PIN in public.

Three ways banks deploy these terminals

Inside the branch, beside the counter. The terminal takes statement printing, balance enquiry, PIN change and card activation off the teller line. The teller keeps account opening and advice, which is the work that actually needs a person. This is the lowest-risk deployment because the unit stands in a supervised area and the queue is already managed.

In a 24-hour lobby. The same functions, but the unit is now alone with the public overnight. That changes the specification: bolt-down plinth, vault-style service door, alarm contacts, anti-peep surround and a screen recessed into the fascia rather than sitting flush. A card-only build is normal here; a cash build is a different project.

In an agency or mini-branch. Space is a corridor, not a hall. The unit has to fit a footprint of about a metre and still carry a PIN pad, card readers and a printer. This is where the wall-mounted and slim cabinets earn their place, and where the mounting height is decided by the room rather than by a standard.

The common failure across all three is specifying the cabinet before the payment device. A PIN pad that is not yet approved, or a card reader that arrives with a different bezel, forces a re-cut fascia — the most expensive kind of change in a small programme.

Specification points that decide the project

PIN ENTRY

The PIN pad is never integrated

An encrypting PIN pad is a separately certified device. It sits in its own module bay with a privacy hood, and it can be swapped for a bank-qualified model without re-drawing the cabinet.

PRIVACY

Recess, hood, orientation

A portrait screen with a recessed face and a hooded keypad lets the next customer stand behind the user's shoulder without seeing the screen. Queue direction is part of the drawing.

CERTIFICATION

Per market, per device

Payment certification follows the device and the acquirer, not the cabinet. A build that is compliant in one market is not automatically compliant in another, so the destination country belongs in the enquiry.

CASH

Cash changes the cabinet

A cash acceptor or recycler needs depth, a secure fascia and a service procedure. Card-only builds are smaller, cheaper and faster to certify; cash should be a deliberate choice.

SECURITY

Bolt-down and alarm-ready

For unsupervised lobbies, the plinth has a bolt-down option and the cabinet carries mounting points for an alarm contact or security seal supplied by your contractor.

SERVICE

Who opens it, and how

Paper and module access should be a front operation that branch staff can perform. If a service visit is needed every time the printer runs out, the unit will sit empty instead.

Procurement checklist

  • The PIN pad model and its approval status in the target market
  • The payment device model, bezel drawing and mounting footprint
  • Which services the terminal will offer, in the order the customer meets them
  • Whether the site is supervised, or open to the public overnight
  • Cash handling: none, acceptor only, or acceptor plus recycler
  • Queue layout, and the clear space in front of the screen
  • Cleaning and first-line maintenance: who changes paper, who resets a jam
  • Destination country, because it sets the certification path

Questions we are asked first

Can we fit our own certified PIN pad instead of the supplied unit?
Yes. The PIN pad sits in a separate module bay rather than being moulded into the cabinet, so a bank-qualified device can be installed once the enclosure drawing and mounting footprint are agreed. Send the model and its installation drawing with the enquiry.
Is a card-only terminal cheaper and faster to deploy than a cash build?
Usually, on both counts. A card-only cabinet is smaller, needs no cash-handling procedure and has a narrower certification scope. Cash acceptance or recycling adds depth, a secure fascia and a service process, so it is quoted as a defined variant.
How much space does the terminal need in front of it?
As a planning guide, about 1.2 m of clear space and a queue line behind the user's shoulder. Portrait orientation and the hooded PIN pad are chosen for that arrangement, but the final figure comes from your own branch standard.
Can the kiosk print statements on A4 instead of a receipt slip?
The reference builds carry an 80 mm thermal printer. An A4 module can be added inside the larger cabinets, which changes the internal layout and the service access, so it is quoted as a cabinet variant rather than an option.
